**Katagiri Roshi:**

> 13.
>
> Those assembled in the hall should not wear brocade but rather things like
paper robes. Those who understood the way in the past were all like this.
>
> – From “Regulations for the Auxiliary Cloud Hall (Jūundō-shiki)” in *Moon in a Dewdrop*, edited by Kazuaki Tanahashi.

“Those assembled in the hall shouldn’t wear brocade, but rather things like paper robes.” 

I think in history they seem to wear paper robes, tiny thick robes which were pretty strong, because they were very poor. So they never wore silk or brocade. Maybe there were paper robes and also cotton. But in the modern age, I think, there is the synthetic fiber, and also silk. We are very, in a sense, luxurious. 

But basically, particularly for monks, when it was at Eiheiji monastery, we couldn’t wear silk, et cetera. So cotton clothes, kimono, cotton robes. That was allowed for monks to wear. But recently, pretty [unintelligible]. Anyway, we should remember how the ancestors practiced. 

“Those who understood the way in the past were all like this.” 

Anyway the, not the sensible alternative to be poor or to be rich. A basic teaching of spiritual life is, I think, [to be] poor. But that poor is not a sense of alternative, to be poor or to be rich. In a sense, there is no alternative. If you become a very serious religious person, very naturally people become very poor. 

In China, I don’t think they liked very much wearing the paper robe. But if you seek for the spiritual life, very naturally you become poor, because under the situation of the poor, very naturally you can understand human feelings, emotions, human life. You can really understand human life under unfavorable conditions. But that don’t mean you should torture yourself, or you should force yourself to have grief, et cetera. It must be natural for spiritual seekers. 

So, the important point is that it is because a spiritual person must live, must be alive always, under all circumstances, under the favorable conditions or under the unfavorable conditions. That is spiritual persons, religious persons responsibility, because they have to help human beings. If you always live under favorable conditions, you cannot help human beings, you cannot save human beings from their suffering. You don’t understand human suffering, human pain, hard work, et cetera. 

So, anyway, a spiritual person has to stand up always, anytime, anywhere, under difficult conditions, under not difficult conditions. That’s why they sometimes practice like this. Not sometimes; they practice like this. 

But we are living in the very nice modern human society. I don’t know whether this is lucky for us or not. 

Anyway, I shouldn’t tell you that you should be always poor or you should be rich. It depends on you. 

#### 7:37

> 14.
>
> Do not enter the hall intoxicated with wine. If you do so by accident you should make formal repentance. Do not have wine brought into the hall. Do not enter the hall smelling of leeks.
>
> – From “Regulations for the Auxiliary Cloud Hall (Jūundō-shiki)” in *Moon in a Dewdrop*, edited by Kazuaki Tanahashi; modified by Katagiri Roshi.

And do not enter the hall intoxicated with wine. Of course, you understand, this is very important. Just like driving a car: you cannot drive a car with alcoholic intoxication. You cannot do it. 

But in a sense, apparantly you can do with sober mind, but inside is really intoxicating. The same applies to driving a car. If you drink wine and alcohol, very strong alcohol and wine a lot, and also if you are very strong drinker, for a while you are intoxicated, but if you continue to drink, you can overcome intoxication, and apparantly you are very calm, very calm. But inside is completely intoxicated. If you see such a person you don’t understand whether he is drunk or not. But if you let him do something, driving a car, completely that’s crazy driving. This is very common. 

So, if you drink and also are sitting zazen, apparantly you can do zazen quietly, but you cannot do zazen *exactly*. So that’s why it says here, “Do not drink alcohol intoxicated with wine.” 

“But if you do so by accident” – the original text it says literally... let me translate it literally. He says, “If you unwittingly forget and make a mistake.” He says, “You should make formal repentance”. [So it’s] very kind, compassionate. This is a rule completely, but he understands human beings. So that’s why he says, “If you *unwittingly* forget and make a mistake, you should make formal repentance.” 

[...] This formal repentence means three bows to Manjushri after breakfast, and you should apologize to Manjushri and all monks. That is formal repentance. 

“Do not have wine brought into the hall.” 

Of course. You cannot drink alcohol brought into that hall. 

“Do not enter the hall smelling of leeks.”

In the monastery we have completely vegetarian dishes. So in the monastery we don’t use leeks, garlic, green onion or usual onion, pepper. And also rocambole – kind of a wild onion. And chives. Anything like this. So, we don’t use these vegetables which have very strong smells and very strong stimulation. 

That’s why he says, “Do not enter the hall smelling of leek or garlic”. 

#### 14:13

> 15.
>
> Quarreling persons should go out of the hall, because it hinders not only their own work in the way but also that of others. Those who see such quarreling and do not stop it are equally at fault.
>
> – From “Regulations for the Auxiliary Cloud Hall (Jūundō-shiki)” in *Moon in a Dewdrop*, edited by Kazuaki Tanahashi.

So, quarreling persons should go out of the hall because it hinders not only their own work in the way, but also that of others. 

When I was at Eiheiji monastery, all the monks were very wild and very rough. Because it was right after the Second World War, so almost 60 percent of the monks came back from the Second World War. So they were very rough, and wild. Pretty often they were fighting each other. Next day, they were kicked out of the monastery. 

Because the monastery is sangha, a particular place where we have to establish a peaceful life, a peaceful world, showing a good example to all sentient beings. That is a monastery; that is a sangha. That’s why if you quarrel and fight in that situation, of course it is not right. So at any cost, we should live in peace and harmony. That is the most important. Whatever reason there. That’s why in the beginning Dogen Zenji says, “The assembly in the hall should be harmonious like milk and water and promote the activity of the way.” If you live in peace and harmony, then you can really promote [or] further the Buddha way. But if you fight each other, intellectually or emotionally or psychologically, it is not good. It is really interrupting, bothering, destroying the Buddha way. So you have to be very careful. Anyway, live in peace and harmony. 

So, that’s why you have to always focus on Buddha’s way, Buddha’s world, not the samsaric world. I told you this during this training. If you always stay in the samsaric world and think something, try to find a solution, you cannot find a perfect solution. No. You cannot do it not good. And also you cannot practice the Buddha way in such a situation. So, you have to completely [comport] yourself; be right in the middle of the universe, Buddha’s way. Very constantly you should do it. 

Whatever you think, “it is effective” or “it is not effective,” anyway, while you are in the monastery, practice in the Buddha way, you have to be constantly in the Buddha way. *[unintelligible]*, when you think, when you act, when you imagine, when you deal with, when you see your emotional or human problems, anyway, you have to see everything in terms of the Buddha way. That is most important. 

“Those who see such quarreling and do not stop it are equally at fault.”

This is also fault. Because directly or indirectly, you participate in fighting. So that is really bothering others. So you try to stop fighting. That’s why here it says, “Those who see such quarreling and do not stop it are equally at fault.”

#### 19:54

> 16.
>
> Those who do not follow the admonitions of the hall should be removed. And those who have the same mind with the perpetrators are also at fault.
>
> – From “Regulations for the Auxiliary Cloud Hall (Jūundō-shiki)” in *Moon in a Dewdrop*, edited by Kazuaki Tanahashi; modified by Katagiri Roshi.

“Those who do not follow the admonitions of the hall should be removed.”

This is, I told you very often, the monastic rules or regulations come from the understanding of the depths of human life; [they are] not given by somebody. And also the monks practice in the Buddha way, standing always in the depths of human life, not the samsaric world. So if you don’t follow the monastic rules, it’s really a perpetrator, so you should be removed. You should be leave, or you should move out. 

And also, “those who have the same mind as the perpetrators are also at fault.”

“The same mind as the perpetrator” means accomplice. If you don’t follow this, this is exactly perpetrator, and then if you don’t give a piece and if you don’t help, it means in a sense, directly or indirectly, you participate in these ways. So very naturally you become and accomlice. This is also fault, in the monastery.

So, we together help each other, mutually help each other. But don’t fight; at any cost we try to be find peaceful way. Not only one person, anyway, together, we try to [be] together, and help. That is important. 

Personally if you do this, very often you create trouble. So, we are careful, we have to give a piece of advice to the people. Because personally, everyone has arrogance, and consciously or unconsciously proud of him or her self. Particularly if you have [plenty of] experience of practice of monastic life, you become arrogant, very naturally. 

#### 24:11

> 17.
>
> Do invite priests or lay people into the hall, as this may agitate the assembly. Do not speak loudly with guests near the hall. And do not talk about practice in a self-praising way, in order to get offerings. However, those who have a long-standing intention to practice or those who are on pilgrimage may be allowed inside. In such cases you should always consult the director of the hall beforehand.
>
> – From “Regulations for the Auxiliary Cloud Hall (Jūundō-shiki)” in *Moon in a Dewdrop*, edited by Kazuaki Tanahashi; modified by Katagiri Roshi.

“Do not invite priests or lay people into the hall as this may agitate the assembly.”

You shouldn’t invite even a priest or even lay people, because you agitate the assembly. Because all monks must live in samadhi, tranquility and dynamism, in the Buddha’s world, not in the samsaric world. In the Buddha’s world, samadhi is constantly you can stay in the Buddha way, dharma world, or truth, which is called tranquility. Simultaneously, you have to act, you have to live there. This is a practice in the monastery, but if you invite a stranger, even a priest, or lay people, people are surprised to see a stranger in the hall. So that is agitating; you really agitate people. It’s not a good attitude. 

So as best as we can, we should keep the circumstances of Buddhist monks practice in peace and harmony, just like the water of the river going constantly.

“Do not speak loudly with guests near the hall.”

This is also very important. 

Not only speaking loudly; before Dogen Zenji said, talking loudly and also blowing the nose loudly, and walking carelessly with the [pit pat]. 

When you walk inside the zendo you try to walk quietly, but if you walk around the zendo you are really careless. But when you come to the zendo, before you get into the zendo, that is also practice for you. Walking in the hallway, walking outside near the zendo, this is also inside of the zendo. So you have to walk quietly; you shouldn’t walk with a pit pat, pit pat. 

That’s why he says “do not speak loudly with guests near the hall.”

“And do not talk about practice in a self-praising way, in order to get offerings.”

This happens often. In Eiheiji monastery, people believe in Eiheiji monastery is where very strict practice is going on. So the monks individually really talk about how strict practice does. In a sense, it’s self-praising. So, he says, “do not talk about practice in a self-praising way in order to get offerings.” Particularly the people who are related with [Buddha], very naturally we say so. That is, consciously or unconsciously, the mind works on currying favor with people. You are very friendly, you are intimate with, talking about strict practice, et cetera, to get praise or to get – not only material offerings, but also mental offerings. 

So during the sesshin I mentioned, I don’t think you should talk about your own things so much. This is my excuse. If you talk about your own things, whatever good or bad, *[unintelligible]*. So as best as you can should talk about [yourself] in public. Because people misunderstand you. And also, unconsciously, personally, you don’t notice the very minute vibrartion of human consciousness to have self-praising, and putting down some other things. Psychologically, that always happens. 

“However, those who have a long-standing intention to practice or those who are on pilgrimage may be allowed inside.”

That is, I told you before I think, if there are some individuals who have long-standing intention to practice means there are some individuals who really want to have opportunity to see, to meet the monks practicing in the zendo. So at that time, you should get permission from the director of the hall beforehand, and you can chant the sutra in the zendo. You need to invite those people inside the zendo. 

“In such cases you should always consult the director of the hall beforehand.”

#### 32:50

> 18.
>
> You should practice zazen in this hall just as in the monks' hall. Never neglect the morning practice or the evening request for instruction.
>
> – From “Regulations for the Auxiliary Cloud Hall (Jūundō-shiki)” in *Moon in a Dewdrop*, edited by Kazuaki Tanahashi; modified by Katagiri Roshi.

“You should practice zazen in this hall”: “This hall” means the rear zazen hall. I told you before, there were two zazen halls in those days, the front zazen hall and rear zazen hall. So in this case, the rear zazen hall. 

I don’t know, maybe rear zazen hall was used maybe by the... I don’t know how,  in those days. In Eiheiji monastery recently we have two zazen halls; one is on top of the hill, the other one is at the bottom of the hill, open to visitors and lay buddhists who want to practice there. So maybe we can call the other one on the bottom of the hill, open to the lay buddhists et cetera, could be called the rear zazen hall. But here it says you should practice zazen in rear zazen hall just as in the monks’ hall. Maybe the rear zazen hall might be used for the people who are interested in doing zazen, separate from the monks’ zazen hall. I don’t know exactly. 

So that’s why here it says, just as in the monks hall. So even the people who practice in the rear zazen hall should follow the same way of practice as the monks who practice in the front zazen hall. 

“Never neglect the morning practice or the evening request for instruction.” 

“Morning practice” is called *chōsan* (朝參). The evening request for instruction is called *boshō* (暮請). I don’t know exactly now; I couldn’t tell you definitely the difference between the *chōsan* and *boshō*. Maybe a little difference between, but I cannot tell you now, because I have to check. 

But anyway, generally speaking, *chōsan* and *boshō*, the morning practice or the evening request for instruction, are to see the abbot, from whom you can have a piece of advice. For your practice, for your understanding, after breakfast, after dinner, there is *chōsan* or *boshō*.

> 19.
>
> At meal time, those who drop eating bowls or utensils on the floor should be fined according to the regulations of the monastery.
>
> – From “Regulations for the Auxiliary Cloud Hall (Jūundō-shiki)” in *Moon in a Dewdrop*, edited by Kazuaki Tanahashi; modified by Katagiri Roshi.

 At mealtime, those who drop eating bowls or utensils on the floor, because the buddha bowls are the not the usual utensils we use every day. Buddha bowl is the symbol of Buddha’s head, Buddha’s body. So, we have to deal with the Buddha bowl very carefully, in the same way as dealing with the Buddha’s head, the Buddha’s body. That’s why the Dogen Zenji says, “those who drop eating bowls or utensils on the floor should be fined.” 
 
 “Fine” doesn’t mean the usual fine; originally it says “cleaning the oil lamp.” Very old style of oil lamp. One day or all night, we have to clean all the lamps used in the monastery. This is a fine, if you drop eating bowls or utensils. We apologize to Manjusri [...] and we have to polish all the lamps. 
 
 So, that is a fine. According to the regulations of the monastery; this is the regulation of the monastery. 
 
Sometimes if you fight, sometimes you have to have this kind of fine: to polish the lamps all day. Because lots of lamps there. Now we have electricity, so we don’t have lamps. But in ancient times, they used lamps, so if you try to polish, it really takes time.

> 20.
>
> The admonitions of buddhas and ancestors should always be followed. The pure rules of the monastery are to be inscribed on your bones and mind.
>
> – From “Regulations for the Auxiliary Cloud Hall (Jūundō-shiki)” in *Moon in a Dewdrop*, edited by Kazuaki Tanahashi.

“The admonition of the buddhas and ancestors should always be followed” because “the pure rules of the monastery are to be inscribed on your bones and mind.” This is not the usual regulation given by somebody, like morality. These rules and regulations of the monastery should be something which real life penetrates your body, your skin, your muscles, your bones, and marrow. This is a [need]. If you don’t, you cannot have the opportunity to deepen your life. So, the reason is to deepen, to enhance your life. If you deepen your life, very naturally you can help [...]. That is a [need]. 

That’s why “admonition of the buddhas and ancestors” – because buddhas and ancestors lived like this. So, whether you don’t like or you like, completely beyond this, anyway, you should follow. What’s why in the monastery we [use this]. 

And particularly, you always have a question of the personal relation with the teacher. I don’t know, I cannot tell you now. You should find what is personal relation between teacher and [...]. 

There is a good example. [...] Zen Master, one of the abbots of Eiheiji monastery. He lived in 19th century. But he couldn’t [...] every day. In the modern sense, he might be [...]. But anyway, he needed to have [...] every day. And his younger brother was very mad at him, because [] Zenji was always drunk, and [...] so much. One day, he made a mischief, hanging the [...] from his neck while he slept. By leaving the death and anyway, in that time...

*[Tape change.]* 

... He died in 1853. He was very strict, pay attention, particularly to his disciples. 

And then unfortunately, he passed by his room and found the [Kankei] Zenji with a sake bottle hanging from his neck. So immediately he ran into his room and gave twelve blows to Kankei Zenji. But he didn’t understand, at the moment he struck, hit kysoku ... But anyway, his teacher gave 20 blows. But he didn’t say anything; just received 20 blows. He was pretty well noticed for the typical Zen monk who was audacious, was very undaunted, and showed great devotion to his teacher. He never talked back for whatever reason. 

In common sense, you don’t understand this. Because in common sense, you see this kind of story as a very senseless way of dealing with human beings. But in the spiritual sense, it is natural. 

I don’t think it is not kind of a marriage with your teacher. 

In San Francisco, somebody called me. He really married with his teacher. And then something happened. He was really confused. I looked at the marriage with the teacher. Because still there is [...], and also attachment there. And also if you are married with your teacher, naturally you want to set the teacher on a pedestal. And the teacher also becomes put on the pedestal. And forgetting completely to temper religious craziness, [...]. 

So you have to completely devote to the teacher, but [it’s] not marriage. Because if your are marreid, very naturally there is human attachment. Attachment makes you crazy; you don’t see exactly the person in the broad sense, because you are attached to the only one. Just like a mother and child. You really love your child. But, because you have a very strong attachment, completely beyond human speculation. 

So, not married with the teacher. Full devotion is you should devote to the teacher, but simultaneously you must be always sober. You cannot be intoxicated. 

So you shouldn’t deal with the teaching or teacher kind of as just a human being. You cannot do it either. Because if you see the teacher always just as a human being, you cannot see the Buddhist aspect, the great nature of the human beings. 

So basically you have to stand up constantly in the Buddha’s world. Buddha of teacher, Buddha of the disciple. Practically, that means complete respect, total respect. And then you have to act in that level; total respect. And then you can devote yourself to the teacher. 

But on the other hand, when you devote, you show your devotion, very naturally devotion shows various ways. Various discriminations, different problems come up. So, if you show the devotion, real devotion, that devotion is not pure exactly. But basically you constantly stand up in the pure sense of the human world, so-called Buddha. Deep respect, anyway, under all circumstances. And then you show the devotion, but the devotion shown by you, is not pure. That’s why there is a little bit complicated situation. So you have to reflect upon your devotion. And also the teacher should be careful for teacher to accept full devotion. Constantly there is something to sober each other. You must be sober; you cannot be intoxicated. For this, your practice must be a very serious, in a sense.

So, in the practice level, two things work together. Completely respect, beyond human speculation. But on the other hand, devotion shown in the dualistic world is not pure, so you have to reflect on your devotion, and the teacher has to reflect upon the devotion shown by the students. [...] So, simultaneously, those two work together. 

Well, it’s possible. You can do it. 

So, in a sense, sometimes this kind of practice is not something you can get always merit. It’s not merit, not the benefit, in a sense. In common sense, it’s very difficult to have usual benefit through this. But for long run, you should really benefit. But for a certain period of time, you cannot get the benefit. Because, so much pain. Which you have to [...], which is [...]. 

A Zen Master in Japan, he says to monks, “If you have a beneficial action or beneficial result, it’s not good for you. If you want to be a really good monk, you shouldn’t have a benefit.” It means you cannot get the benefit. That’s pretty harsh. But for long run, you really benefit. 

But that’s why the teacher has to be very careful, [deal with] very carefully. Because [it’s a] very unreasonable situation in which the teacher and disciple have to live. And educate each other, and transmit real buddha’s dharma. It’s not the teaching; it is something you have to learn through the actual living teacher and [...]. 

So, if you have always feeling good from the teacher’s way, it’s not good. That’s why very strict teaching is very good for you. But we don’t like it. 

So, that the monastic rules. Pure rules; that’s why he says, “Pure rules of the monastery are to be inscribed on your bones and mind.”
