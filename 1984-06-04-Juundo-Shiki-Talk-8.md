> 7.
>
> When carrying out either important matters or trifles, you should always consult with the director of the hall. Those who do things without consulting with the director of the hall should leave. If you neglect the ritual of guest and host, you can understand neither the true nor the phenomenal.
>
> – From “Regulations for the Auxiliary Cloud Hall (Jūundō-shiki)” in *Moon in a Dewdrop*, edited by Kazuaki Tanahashi; modified by Katagiri Roshi.

**Katagiri Roshi:** I told you before the director of the hall is the Ino. So whatever you want to do, you want to carry out, small or moderate, you should always talk with the director of the hall, and then you should start to do that. Otherwise it’s very difficult to maintain order in the monastic life. 

The relationship between the Ino and the monks [is that the] Ino is Ino, monks are monks. So we should respect the position of the Ino and the position of the monks, and we should help each other. But [it is] not a combination, not mixed up. The position of the Ino is the position of the Ino. The position of the monks is the position of the monks. But they are communicating. That is really a matter of practice, not discussion. So that’s why we should constantly contact with the Ino and do something. 

> Those who do things without consulting with the director of the hall should leave. 

“Leave” means to change the task. 

In a big monastery, mainly the structure of the monastery is divided into three sections. One section is the practice and education section, and the second is the administrative section, and the third one is the business section. That is a very broad outline. So, education and practice section, and administrative section, and business section. 

And all the monks in each section have their own bording room. That bording room is not an individual bording room; at each bowling room several monks live, sleep. And then one group of the monks takes care of one task. So, in each section, several bording rooms where five or six monks lay down, and they take care of several tasks. 

So, I think “leave” doesn’t meansto get out of the monastery, just to change the task or to change the section. Do you understand what I mean? 

So, “Those who do things without consulting with the director of the hall should leave.” I think here we are talking about the regulation of the *sōdō* (meditation hall), belonging to education and practice section. So, if there are some monks who don’t follow the suggestion of the Inos, then they must be changed to another section, administrative section or business section. The Ino should decide, anyway. That is “leave.” 

> If you neglect the ritual of guest and host, you cannot understand the relationship between the guest and the host.

“Guest and host” means, the “guest” means the monks, and “the host” means the director of the hall. So that means the relationship between guest and host, the director of the hall and the monks. That means superior and inferior, in other words. 

So if you don’t accept this rule, you cannot keep maintaining the situation in order; it’s messed up. So that makes the life confused. That’s why the Ino’s position is Ino, which everyone should respect. 

That’s why here it says, “If you neglect the ritual of guest and host, you cannot understand the relationship between the guest and the host.”

That means, well, I think in terms of *tathagatagarbha*, the life in the reality consists of the two aspects: discrimination and equality. So in [a sense] discrimination is phenomenon, and equality is the truth. 

So the life in reality called tathagatagarbha consists of unity of discrimination and equality. And in the discrimination, there are different situations: ino, monk, administrator, and businessmen. And you cannot always deal with the peoples and the sections and human life just in terms of discrimination. At that time you always compete with the people, with the comparison of the human power, manpower, always. Do you understand? Always you try. That is the world of discrimination. Because you just try to take care of human life in terms of discrimination. So, it’s impossible to do that. So we have to deal with human life in terms of equality too. 

So, unity of equality and discrimination is not a matter of discussion. We have to show unity of the discrimination and equality *in actual practice*. That is pretty hard; but it’s not pretty hard: you just take care of the ino as he really is. So, basically, we have to have a very deep respect. Constantly we have to have a pretty deep respect to ino position. 

That is equality. 

And then, at that time the advice from the ino can be accepted easily, because you respect deeply. But if you don’t, if a piece of advice that comes from the ino, you immediately [...]. So, it’s not a good attitude. So very naturally confusion that comes up. 

So that’s why here it says you cannot understand the relationship between the host and the guest. 

If you understand, that is not a hierarchy. In the monastery, there is a very strict attitude toward human life based on respect to superiors; teacher and disciples. Disciples completely respect teachers. That is a little bit a big problem for you; what is so-called full devotion to the teacher? So you don’t understand this one, but I think you should learn this full devotion toward the superior in terms of actual practice, one. The other one is, you should understand the background for this actual practice; why you have to totally respect the teacher. Because it is not a matter of discussion; you should learn it through the actual practice. 

Maybe you have questions [about this], but [let’s answer them] later. I want to [read the next part].

> Inside or near the hall, do not put your heads together and talk loudly. The
director should prohibit this.
>
> – From “Regulations for the Auxiliary Cloud Hall (Jūundō-shiki)” in *Moon in a Dewdrop*, edited by Kazuaki Tanahashi.

Near the zendo, the zazen hall, you shouldn’t talk loudly. Particularly when the monks practice zazen, you cannot talk loudly near to the zendo. So that’s why it says, “Inside or near the hall, do not put your heads together and talk loudly.” So, you cannot talk to the inside of the zendo. “The director should prohibit this.”

> Do not do *kinhin* in the hall.
>
> – From “Regulations for the Auxiliary Cloud Hall (Jūundō-shiki)” in *Moon in a Dewdrop*, edited by Kazuaki Tanahashi; probably modified by Katagiri Roshi.

In the ancient times, we had a *kinhin* (walking meditation) passage [...]. The *kinhin* place is set up outside of the zendo, like this. But this *kinhin* passage is not actually inside the house. 

**Someone:** It is enclosed? 

**Katagiri Roshi:** The passage is closed, with windows and walls. It isn’t exposed to wind and snow and rain. So any time you can use this kinhin passage. 

This is the rule of a monastery in ancient times. So, do not do *kinhin* in the hall.

> 10.
>
> Do not hold or carry beads in the hall. Do not enter or leave with your hands
hanging down.
>
> – From “Regulations for the Auxiliary Cloud Hall (Jūundō-shiki)” in *Moon in a Dewdrop*, edited by Kazuaki Tanahashi.

“Do not hold or carry beads in the hall” means the rosary. 

Most of people don’t understand the rosary. [...] In the [Shin] school, they use rosary very often, [or] always. Their idea is to eliminate 108 delusions. You do like this, [...] counting the beads. Tibetan priests always [have] mala beads.

So, I want to say a few things. [I’m thinking of] a proverb. In ancient times, there was an epidemic. It spread with tremendous force in Magadha in India. So, people were completely disgusted with this tremendous spread. So the people in Magadha asked Shakyamuni Buddha what to do. So Buddha says, “Make a rosary, make a ring of beads, and count the beads. Each time when you count one bead, you chant the name of Buddha.” Do you understand? So, maybe he suggested a hundred times; a hundred times we should chant the name of Buddha, each time you count one bead. That is the original story, which we started to use the rosary. 

But, the structure of the rosary is a little bit different now. Have you ever seen a rosary? You can see the big beads, bottom and top. The bottom is the symbol of Buddha Shakyamuni. That is the Buddha. And, crossing the ring, at the top, that is another big one, that is the symbol of bodhisattva. So, Buddha and bodhisattva. And, on the right side and the left side, on each side, there are two small beads. [...] So, four beads are the symbol of guardians, called Shitenno (四天王), guardians for protecting buddha-dharma. And the four guardians, Shitenno, come from the story of the ancient Indian outlook on the human world. At the top of Mount Sumeru is the place where one god stays – I forget the Sanskrit; in Japanese it’s Taishakuten (Indra). And then around this top of the mountain, at four corners, there are four guards. Those are protecting buddha-dharma, living at the four corners. That is the legendary story, of the ancient outlook on the human world. So that’s why in the rosary also, there are four beads and the one bead. And then in between those beads, seventeen small beads are there. So in all, 108 beads. 

But, anyway. So the origin of the rosary is used for the counting the beads when you chant the name of Buddha, when you chant the sutra again and again. For instance, in ancient times, on particular days in the monastery, they chant the short sutras a hundred times. At that time, the leader of the ceremony carried the rosary under their sleeve – not shown outside, under the sleeve – and the leader of the ceremony always counted. And also the doan carried the rosary beads under the sleeve and counted. So, in ancient times, the leader of the ceremony and the doan always carried the rosary. And, in order to count the beads, when they chant repeatedly the sutra or name of Buddha. 

That is one reason. And, also, a second reason is, you know we chant when we have a ceremony, morning service and afternoon service. We bow three times first, and then hit the three bells, the big bell, and then we start the chanting. The three bells, I told you before, the purpose of the big bell is to prepare for chanting. To have space, and to have calm mind, to prepare for the chanting. 

I want to say a little more about this. The purpose of hitting the big bell three times is to invite the Buddha from, anyway, I want to say, samadhi of zazen. Buddha is always in samadhi, constantly samadhi. That is a buddha. Well in this case, trees, birds, and grasses, the all sentient beings are exactly right in the middle of samadhi. Very quiet and also active. Without any discrimination. This is the state of samadhi. So, Buddha is constantly maintaining himself just like that. So we ask the Buddha to stand up from the feet of the samadhi, and then participate in our ceremony. So in order to do this, we hit the bell to awaken him. Can you understand? Please wake up, and please come and join us. 

And then, before you hit the bell, we have three bows together. That is, before you ask him, very quietly we prostrate to the Buddha, before you invite him. And then next, we hit the bell, and then we ask. [...] Please, join us. 

So, during the hitting the bell, the leader of the ceremony bows three times. Actually, you should bow; you should bow with me, with the leader of the ceremony. Because, when we site, I offer the incense, and then you hit the bell, and then Buddha wakes up and join us; time to join us. So, I come back here and bow. That is [that] we see the Buddha in this service. So, very naturally, Buddha comes; so, we bow. Naturally we should bow together. But only the leader of the ceremony bows three times.

But, if you don’t have the big bell, the leader of the ceremony carries the rosary under the sleeve, and rubs the rosary, like this, making sounds to wake up the Buddha. Have you ever seen this? 

But, people don’t understand this; they don’t understand why they do it like this. Just showing, demonstrating. But it has a meaning, because we don’t have a bell, so please, Buddha, wake up and join us. That is using the rosary. 

So actually, basically, the leader of the ceremony and the doan carry some beads, rosaries. But otherwise, it’s not necessary to carry the rosary. 

But most of the people believe rosary is a symbol of Buddhism. You know? 

Or, I don’t know, the Zen students in San Francisco carried the rosary, always. But in the *Shobogenzo* chapter of “*Ango*” – *ango* means participation in training period – it says, “It is a rude manner to meet the Buddha with a rosary.” And also, *Shuryo Shingi*, which is one of the chapters in the works by Dogen, “Regulation of Eiheiji Monastery”, he says almost the same thing: it is a rude manner to meet a person with a rosary. Because the purpose of the rosary is counting the beads when you chant the Buddha’s name. But when you see a person, most important thing is you have to *see* the person, you have to meet the person and communicate with them. Not something else. But if you carry the rosary, that means you already tried to communicate with the Buddha. So you ignore the person you meet now. So,that’s why it’s rude, a rude manner. 

So you should know the purpose of the rosary, why you use the rosary. 

With the development of Buddhism, the meaning of the rosary has been changed. That’s why we always carry the rosary if you become a Buddhist, and when you have a wedding service, sometimes priests give a rosary to you as a Buddhist ceremony. This [...] rosary to mind, in my understanding. I don’t mean to say it is wrong, but I don’t think it is necessary. 

So, if you chant the sutras a hundred times, or chant the name of Buddha a hundred times, I really think it is necessary to carry the rosary. In ancient times, every day they did it that way, chanting the sutra a hundred and eight times, on a particular day. That’s why the leader of the ceremony or doan carried always the rosary. 

Is that alright? 

I have a rosary. But I don’t carry the rosary so much. So, I carry the rosary as only at the ordination ceremony, because they use a big bell in the beginning. So, I carry the rosary in the ordination ceremony. So, instead of the big bells, you can use the rosary as a demonstration, inviting the Buddha to join us. 

So that’s why it says, “Do not hold or carry beads in the hall.” Particularly in the zendo, it’s not necessary to carry them. 

 >Do not enter or leave with your hands hanging down.
 
Because one reason is the monks always wear robes with long sleeves, so if you walk like this, always you look tired and sleepy, dragging that way. So it’s not a good atmosphere, a good look. 

On the other hand, if you walk like this it seems to be comfortable, [...] but too comfortable to lift something. I always say it like this: if you stand up very comfortable, and carry your hands like this, have a position like this, you don’t pay attention to your hands. 

So, Dogen Zenji says when you serve the meal, you are using the right hand, and the other hand must be touched to your chest like this, and use your right hand, like this. So, it means you should serve the food with the whole body. If you use the right hand, it means to use the whole body. So, left hand also, and using the whole body. That’s why he said left hand should be touched to the chest. Like this, holding like this, and then serve. 

So, that’s why, if you stand up always like this, very naturally you can pay attention to your hands. Exactly. 

So, “Do not enter or leave with your hands hanging down.”

> 11.
>
> Do not chant the names of buddhas or sutras in the hall. 
>
> – From “Regulations for the Auxiliary Cloud Hall (Jūundō-shiki)” in *Moon in a Dewdrop*, edited by Kazuaki Tanahashi.

This is also, in China, there is a combination of zazen and Shin school, so-called chanting the name of Amitabha. So doing zazen, you chant the name of Amitabha. That means Dogen Zenji really didn’t want to do it like that, because you already have combination of two different practices, zazen and chanting the name of Amitabha. That means [it’s a] “part time job.” If you do zazen, you have to do zazen – as complete as you can be. So that’s why, whether loudly or quietly, anyway, you shouldn’t chant the name of buddhas, or sutras, or passages, whatever. 

So during Zazen, we shouldn’t think of the passages from a sutra, or think the name of Amitabha, or chant the name of Amitabha. Anything. Just concentrate on doing zazen, with wholeheartedness. 

> However, this is permitted when supporters request sutra chanting on a particular occasion.

Sometimes the supporters of temples ask the monastery to meet the monks sitting zazen in the zazen hall, offering something. Particularly during sesshin at Eiheiji monastery, people offered food to the monks sitting in sesshin. So at that time, we have to offer chanting for those people who offer food to us. So, offer the incense, and chanting the ten names of Buddha, and during the chanting those people just walk along the zendo with gassho, bowing. 

So that is a particular occasion. But, nevertheless, we should get the Ino’s permission. So, that’s why it says here, “this is permitted when supporters request sutra chanting on a particular occasion.” 

Or sometimes, even though they don’t offer the food, they really want to meet the monks sitting in zazen, very sincerely, religiously. At that time, first of all you should get the Ino’s permission, and if you get the permission from the Ino, we can invite the supporter... 

*[Tape change.]*

... for a particular ritual, greeting to the Manjusri [statue] before you join the monks’ life. Whoever you are, even if you are a Zen master, or even if you are abbot of another monastery, or even if you are great lecturer on the *Shobogenzo*, go to Eiheiji monastery, but if they don’t have the ritual of greeting to the Manjusri before they are permitted to enter the zendo, they never can get in. 

That is very strict at Eiheiji monastry. So no one can get in; only the monks. Whoever they are, the monks and the other priests who have already the ritual of greeting to the Manjusri. That is the regulation in Eiheiji monastery.

> 12.
>
> Do not spit, blow your nose, or laugh loudly. You should be sobered by the fact that the work of the way is not yet thoroughly mastered. You should regret the subtle passage of time which is eating away this opportunity for practice of the way. Then you may have the sense of being a fish in a small puddle.
>
> – From “Regulations for the Auxiliary Cloud Hall (Jūundō-shiki)” in *Moon in a Dewdrop*, edited by Kazuaki Tanahashi.

So do not spit, blow your nose, or laugh loudly. I mention this very often.

“You should be sobered by the fact that the work of the way is not yet thoroughly mastered.” If you spit or blow your nose or laugh loudly, or yawn carelessly, without covering your mouth, that is that you don’t understand the practice of the Buddha way. The practice of the Buddha way doesn’t penetrate your body, even though you understand intellectually. So that’s why here it says “you should be sobered by the fact that the work of the way is not yet thoroughly mastered.” 

And next he says, “You should regret the subtle passage of time which is eating away this opportunity for practice of the way.” Originally it says... Here it says “to eat away this opportunity for the practice of the way.” Originally it says “life” where he’s saying “this opportunity” – “the life of the practice of the Buddha way.” Because not the opportunity, this is *life*. Because if I say *opportunity*, this is kind of something objective. But *life of the practice* is totally interaction between the opportunity and you. You use the opportunity; the opportunity helps your life. So at that time that is something alive arises. That is called “life of the practice.” So practice is really something alive. It’s life. 

That’s why Dogen Zenji says you should regret the subtle passage of time which is eating away, you [chew the] life of the practice. If you spit or blow your nose or laugh loudly or yawn carelessly, that is really wasting time, because you eat away the life of practice – the life of *your* practice and also simultaneously others’ practice. Because everyone lives in the stream of time, which is going constantly, so-called impermanence. 

So, that’s why this is not, I always mention the regulation or rules in the monasteries, the order given by the *inos* or given by the abbot. You have to understand through and through why you do this kind of practice. The main reason is that, regardless of whether you are conscious of or not, the purpose of practice is based on realization of impermanence. Anyway, through and through. 

So that’s why in order to have a chance or opportunity to realize practically the impermanence, day by day – for this you should behave or act carefully. For yourself, for others. Because if you interrupt others, simultaneously you take away the people from the opportunity of realizing the impermanence. 

The practice itself is always to put the people right in the middle of realization of impermanence, regardless of whether you are conscious of it or not. This is the practice. That’s why if you act very carelessly, loudly, that is you don’t understand the meaning of practice. You don’t understand why you do practice. You don’t understand the meaning of impermanence. That’s why Dogen Zenji says it like this: you should regret the subtle passage of time. It’s a very subtle passage. Completely no excuse; everyone, all sentient beings, walks on this passage. Which is eating away this opportunity for practicing the way.

“Then you may have a sense of fish in a small puddle.” It’s really so. It’s really so. 

I don’t mean you should be always nervous, thinking or imagining a small fish struggling in a small puddle. I don’t mean this. You must be alive, fresh, refreshing, constantly, in each moment. Each moment is just like a small puddle – but you have to be alive. In order to be alive, anyway, you can’t waste any time. So, we do our best effort. Anyway we should act, we should have a very humble, and kind, compassionate aim, to maintain the realization of impermanence. Consciously or unconsciously. This is the rule in the monastery.

So, the rules in the monastery, the regulations, are coming from the realization of the depth of human existence. So, everyone should be [proud]. Without one’s compulsion, or other’s compulsions. Anyway, no excuse. Anyway, you should be there. Should [practice]. 

Do you have some questions?
