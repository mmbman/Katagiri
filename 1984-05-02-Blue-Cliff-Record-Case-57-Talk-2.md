**Student:** The sutra compares it to a lamp. It’s not the same at the beginning and the middle and the end, but on the other hand, there is something that’s there.  

**Student:** Is it kind of an accumulation of memories and perceptions and thoughts?

**Katagiri Roshi:** Mm-hmm. Mm-hmm. 

So memory, and perceptions, and [formations], and a certain situation of the human life. When can you create these memories and [perceptions] which are very peculiar to you? 

**Student:** When you’re not totally involved in what you are doing, they come back to you. [When] you lose yourself in experience, you are not aware of those qualities. 

**Katagiri Roshi:** Mm-hmm. 

What I want to tell you is... The sense of the self is not a fixed being. If so, it is kind of provisional. Temporary. 

*Provisional being* is kind of what? Can you say? Provisional being is, for example, kind of what? Temporary. 

**Student:** Conditions. 

**Katagiri Roshi:** Conditions.

**Student:** Function?

**Katagiri Roshi:** No, if you take an example, what kind of experience can you have as a provisional or temporary being in your life? 

**Student:** Anger. 

**Katagiri Roshi:** Well, many things, yes. That is a mental pattern, okay? But I am saying to tell me that provisional beings as an example you can have in your everyday life. Except not mental patterns. 

**Student:** Eat my dinner?

**Katagiri Roshi:** Do you understand my question? 

**Student:** No.

**Student:** You’re asking for an example?

**Katagiri Roshi:** Yes, example. 

**Student:** Like a traveler, a person who is a traveler? Is that provisional?

**Katagiri Roshi:** Traveler, yes. 

**Student:** Carpenter? Those kinds of things? 

**Katagiri Roshi:** Carpenter, yes. Those things. Why don’t you pick up any examples? Provisional beings. 

**Student:** Is that an example of provisional being? When you’re at work you’re a carpenter, when you’re at home you are a husband, when you’re here you are a student?

**Katagiri Roshi:** Sure. 

**Student:** Anything that is different is provisional. 

**Katagiri Roshi:** Provisional, yes. 

**Student:** Different from what? 

**Katagiri Roshi:** Different provisional? Provisional beings are different? Maybe my question doesn’t hit the mark. *[He laughs.]*

Well, in the last page of the *Diamond Sutra*, what does it say about the provisional? 

**Student:** A bubble. 

**Katagiri Roshi:** Yes. A bubble. And? 

**Student:** A flash of lightning. 

**Katagiri Roshi:** A flash of lightning.

**Student:** “A cloud, flash of lightning, a phantom, and a dream.”

**Katagiri Roshi:** Phantom! Great. Phantom, dream. Just a bubble. 

**Student:** How can it be provisional all the time? 

**Katagiri Roshi:** It’s changing constantly. Appears, next moment disappears. Temporarily it exists, but next moment disappears. Okay? 

So, the sense of the self is kind of a dream, phantom, bubbles... or a kind of a habit. Alright? A kind of a habit. 

If you understand this, you can see a little bit behind this dream and phantom – there is something behind the dream and phantom which make them possible to be dream and phantom, both. Do you understand? What is this? 

**Student:** Emptiness? 

**Katagiri Roshi:** Emptiness. 

**Student:** Truth? 

**Katagiri Roshi:** Truth. That is called truth. The Ultimate Path. Do you understand? 

So that’s why if you [realize] this sense of the self, which is a dream, bubbles, and phantom, [...] that guides you to step inside, to the truth. Do you understand? Is that clear? 

So it’s possible! 

And then at that time, simultaneously, you can be free from, you can see the broad scale of the world, the vast expanse of the human existence – which makes all sentient beings a dream, or phantom, and bubbles. Just like the sense of the self; just like this. And then at that time, you can really let go of yourself in the vast expanse of existence. 

Just like a horse or cow: you can let go of yourself into that huge expanse of the horse pasture. And then that is the vast expanse of the horse pasture, which makes the sense of the self to create as a phantom, dream, et cetera. 

This is called truth, *Ultimate Path*. That Ultimate Path is completely beyond difficulty and not difficulty. And that Ultimate Path, vast expanse of the existence, which makes all sentient beings exist temporarily, is completely beyond difficulty, or not difficulty. That means beyond words. Silent. Completely silent. 

So that’s why next Chao Chou said, “‘In the heavens and on earth I alone am the Honored One.’” This is, you have to think [about this] in your whole life. But anyway, let me go [on]. 

And then monk said, “This is still picking and choosing.” So still the monk didn’t understand what the Ultimate Path is. The Ultimate Path is completely beyond words. That is silent, quietness, tranquility. But it is working constantly, [forever,] how it makes all sentient beings, the sense of the self, exist as a dream, bubbles, phantom. 

And then all you have to do is, you have to exist there. You have to make every possible effort to live [in] that vast expanse of existence, the universe. Under all circumstances, you have to walk step by step in that world. That is called “In the heavens and on earth, I alone am the Honored One.” 

But this is based on the double structure of spiritual life. One aspect of the double structure of the spiritual life is that through that sense of self, you realize the limit of the sense of the self. In other words, completely the bottom of the sense of the self; what the sense of the self is, constantly. It is not [like] an avocado seed. Finally you come to the certain stage so-called “the sense of the self is completely like a dream or bubbles.” 

That is through individual life: through individual life you can see yourself and simultaneously you can see the limits of the self. If you see the limit of the self, the limit of the self guides you to vastness of the world. Here is the limit, the limit of your life: if you come to this limit, very naturally this door opens and you can go to the other, different world. 

So through your self, you have to understand the sense of the self and you have to see the limits of the self, you have to see the limit of the emotions or whatever it is. Anyway you have to sit and see yourself. At that time, through the self you can learn who you are, and simultaneously within you there is something vast of existence there. 

So the limit of existence, the limit of your life is simultaneously extending to other life. The duties and [ties] and the limit of the life is completely extending into everyone’s [life]. Because [they’re the] same thing. 

So very naturally, that realization of the limit of the sense of the self is nothing; [it is] exactly the vast expanse of existence. That’s it. [It’s] universal life, et cetera. You can see [this]. 

So finally, your self is a small well – but it’s not a small well, it’s extending, expanding, and then it becomes oceans, this *huge* ocean. 

So from this point, first the double structure is to realize yourself, to research yourself, and then you can realize your life includes the universe. Dōgen Zenji says the self is the universe, total universe. Because through your self you can learn who you are, but simultaneously the limit of yourself is not you; it becomes universal. 

And then, if you see universal life, the more you research that limit of the self the more the limit of the self extends to everyone, and you realize you are just like a swimmer in a huge ocean. 

And then if you make a mistake in your life [...] simultaneously within this vast expanse of the existence everything is corrected, adjusted, naturally. So very naturally, your body [begins] to sink, if you have some problem. It’s not bad; [it’s a] good signal, helping your life. If you see something good, your body is moving very smoothly; you can realize. But if you have something wrong, your body is sinking. Whatever [it is] – sinking [or swimming], mistake or not mistake – all are going within the vastness of existence, and also all are adjusted naturally, corrected constantly, there. 

And then – how are they corrected? Very silently. Very silently. 

So that means, broadly speaking, you do something – [for example] you do *gassho*, okay? You do *gassho*, but if you see *gassho* in terms of your sense, it’s called sometimes *it’s wrong*, sometimes *it’s bad*. But this *gassho* is always going... the *gassho* as an object and the practitioner as a subject, all are going in the vastness of existence. And then, just adjusted. If you are wrong *gassho*, naturally adjusted. That means correct. Correct. 

So all you have to do is do *gassho* with your best. Because that is a huge world – not your small will! By the realization of the small will, the small will becomes oceans. And then you realize where you are: you are right in the middle of the oceans. And then your life is supported by oceans, not your small will. At that time, you can do *gassho* with your effort, and simultaneously that effort or practice returns to silence. In other words, that *gassho*, or whatever – cause and effect, the effect is all going back to oceans, melted away. 

So that means, do *gassho* with silence. All you have to do is just do *gassho*. That’s it. That is called *shikan* gassho. 

Because that is great understanding, great wisdom, ok? Practicing a great wisdom, great understanding, you can really see broad oceans, big oceans. And then you practice great understanding, great knowledge, wisdom, every day, right in the middle of *gassho*. That’s it, [that’s] all you have to do. And then this great wisdom, great understanding, returns to that ocean, melted away. That is silence. 

So that is individual practice; so-called *shikan*. 

And socially. You do something, you help somebody. Socially, okay? The second structure [of the] double structure is, you do something, okay? That is, this action, this practice is based on great understanding, great knowledge. And also with great compassion, great practice, you always do *gassho*. Because each *gassho*, do it and return to silence; do *gassho*, return to silence. So on the other hand, you do this *gassho*, simultaneously this *gassho* must be something to help you, to help others. That means this *gassho* is not a *gassho* you do. This *gassho* is silence. That is silence. This gassho is supported, this gassho is practiced by huge expanse of oceans, which is silence. But this silence must be *formed* as something. We have to take that silence in the possession of a *form*. That is called *gassho*. 

So the universe is silence, but silence must be taken in the possession of a *form*. That is individual practice. So you do something, but this *do* is something helpful to you, to others. Because this is very deep practice; deep *gassho*. It’s not *your* gassho, which *you* think. 

So there is a double structure there always. You always do something, but simultaneously all things you do always come back to the silence, melted away [into] oceans. This is your practice. That is *shikan*. 

Simultaneously, in the relation with all sentient beings, that silence must be anyway manifested as a form. Through great understanding, great knowledge, great practice, great compassion, anyway. That is called *Manjushri*. Manjushri is a symbol of the great wisdom, great understanding. And Samantabhadra: Samantabhadra is a symbol of the compassion and great practice. So that’s why with the Shakyamuni Buddha [image], both Manjushri and Samantabhadra are sitting on the both sides of his seat. That is great understanding and wisdom. Great compassion, and also great practice. 

That is silence; so-called *buddha*. Buddha is silence, always, because [it is] truth itself. And then all we have to do is, both bodhisattvas, so-called Manjushri and Samantabhadra, have to do something. That is all of us. All of us are bodhisattvas. 

So all of us are bodhisattvas – when? When do you become a bodhisattva? When you do something in order to express great compassion, great practice, great understanding, great knowledge – at that time, you become Manjushri bodhisattva. 

This is a buddha. That’s why in Buddhist scriptures, always buddhas are there, but buddha doesn’t appear on the surface. Buddha is always quiet, and all bodhisattvas work, operate in the human world. But bodhisattvas are simultaneously buddhas. [They are] not different. 

So that’s why there is a very serious fact of the double structure of the spiritual life there. First of all, one structure [of the] double structure is we have to do something, and all practice is coming back to silence. On the other hand, that silence must be manifested as a form, through the practice of Manjushri – great understanding, great knowledge, great compassion, great practice. 

And then silence is manifested. We have to manifest. That means through your body – this small, tiny body – you have to express something more than your tiny body. Your tiny body is very noisy, don’t you think so? Very noisy. But you have to manifest silence – perfect silence – through this tiny, noisy body. *[He laughs.]* Do you understand? 

That’s why you say “mindfulness,” you know? Anyway, do something – right in the middle of activity you must manifest silence. *How?* Right in the middle of *gassho*. That silence becomes sort of profundity, or subtlety, or deepness of life. 

If you do practice like this, very naturally people are very impressed by your *gassho*, by your attitude. Wherever you may be, this is great [education for] people.

So that is called “I alone am the Honored One in heaven and on earth.” 

And then, thinking that way is still picking and choosing. That’s why, “how stupid you are.” Finally, he cannot say anything. So, he cannot say anything means, speechlessness is completely that the teacher’s answer takes him to the silent world. Even though he doesn’t understand it, silence is a great answer.

So, finally, in terms of my words, I pretty often say: “Keep your mouth shut; just do it.” But this is not the usual sense of “keep your mouth shut, just do it.” It’s a very deep meaning, alright?
