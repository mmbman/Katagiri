***This transcript is in rough draft state***

**Katagiri Roshi:** Bottom line, page 115:

> Now in Song China, both inside and outside of its one hundred and eighty regions, there are uncountable mountain temples and city temples. Many monks abide there, but those who have not seen my late master, old buddha, are many, and those who have seen him are few. Further, even fewer have heard his words, not to mention those who have personally met with him face-to-face. Even fewer have been allowed to enter his chamber. 

“Chamber” means his room.

> Among these few, how many have been allowed to take refuge in the skin, flesh, bones, and marrow, the eyeball and face of my late master?
>
> – From *Moon in a Dewdrop*, edited by Kazuaki Tanahashi.

Dōgen [is talking about] the situation of the Zen Buddhism of those days. His teacher Ju-ching (Rujing) was the most important Zen teacher of those days, who transmitted the buddha-dharma directly and properly. But even though there were many mountain temples and city temples, [still] not many monks, not many people who had seen Ju-ching Zen Teacher, much less the opportunity to listen to his *taisho*, his Buddhist teaching. So that’s where he says, much less “among these few, how many have been allowed to take refuge in the skin, flesh, bones, marrow, the eyeballs and face of my late master.” “Much less there were not many people who took refuge in” means exactly received his life, who completely conceded in the teacher’s life. That is, he says, how many have been allowed to take refuge in the skin, flesh, bones, marrow, the eyeballs and face of my late master. 

Usually people – disciples, monks – are always complaining, and trying to understand Buddhism in terms of their own views, and also their own philosophical viewpoint, and their own knowledge, et cetera. So that’s why, even though they listened to the teacher’s talks, buddha-dharma, but they didn’t understand. So that’s why it was very difficult for them to take, to receive completely, and to respect his flesh, bones, marrow, completely his life. It’s very difficult. Most people just pass by the surface of the teacher’s life. That what he mentions. 

The same applies to Japan. There are many, many uncomfortable temples, and many priests there. But even when I was young, I think [there were] not many people who tried to listen to famous Zen teachers’ talks and Buddha’s teaching. They respected when we talked about him, but actually they didn’t respect practically. 

That always happens. Even in the Buddha Shakyamuni’s age, I think the scripture mentions three million people knew the Buddha Shakyamuni’s presence. Three million didn’t know, didn’t know his presence. And another three million people completely ignored his presence. Whatever age comes, always such things happen. 

And Dōgen Zenji mentions, because his master’s way of Buddhist practices was pretty strict, in a sense, so he mentions like this: 

> My late master, old buddha, did not easily allow monks to join his monastery. He would say, "Those who are accustomed to a lax way-seeking mind cannot stay in this place." 

“Lax way-seeking mind” – do you understand [this]? *[He laughs.]*

So I think he requires monks to have a very strong, deep way seeking mind; very deep aspiration for Buddhist teaching. So he says, “those who are accustomed to a lax way of seeking mind cannot stay in this place.” 

> He would chase them out and say, "What can we do with those who have not realized original self? 

I think the *original self* means how [much] intention do you have; if you want to seek for the truth, how strong intention do you have. That is already first stage of realization of original self. That’s why if you don’t have a strong, serious way seeking mind, he didn’t allow anybody to practice there. Dōgen Zenji actually heard these words, and he really saw the monks stay out from the monastery. 

And the teacher saying, “What can we do with those who haven’t realized original self?” And then next it says, “Such dogs...” *[He laughs.]* Dōgen Zenji uses “dogs”:

> Such dogs stir people up. 

It means such persons are nothing but very noisy, boisterous persons. 

When I was at Eiheiji monastery *[he laughs]*, among the monks there were, not only one, two or three, who were really boisterous, stir up. If you were seriously practicing, they always put me down; you know, “You are crazy,” and that [sort of thing]. So that really interrupted, disturbed monks’ practice. 

So, Dōgen Zenji uses that *dogs*. I think this “dogs,” I don’t know, I couldn’t see this comment, you know, here is a star mark. 

**Someone:** Asterisk.

**Katagiri Roshi:** Yeah. But I don’t know where is this. 

**Someone:** It’s there way in the back.

**Katagiri Roshi:** Did you read that explanation?

**Someone:** It says:

> *dogs*. 狗子 (*kushi*). People who do not understand the way. 

**Katagiri Roshi:** Oh, *kushi*?

You know, the dog usually barking, pretty often. *[He laughs.]*

So they shouldn’t be permitted to join the monastery. 

> I have personally seen and heard this. 

You know, Dōgen Zenji *personally* has seen this situation, and also heard his teacher’s statement towards those monks. 

> I think to myself, "Which roots of unwholesome actions made it impossible for them to abide with my late master even though they were from the same country?”

You know, they were all Chinese, so what kind of unwholesome action did they make, because they were not allowed to. So he thinks to himself; and also he says, 

> “... With what fortune was I allowed not only to join the monastery, but to enter the chamber whenever I wished, to take refuge in his venerable form and to listen to the dharma words, even though I was someone from a remote country?”

So he really appreciated his situation, [in] which [he] was allowed to practice, and to go to his chambers anytime to meet him and discuss the buddha-dharma. So he really felt happy. But on the other hand, he really paid sympathy to those Chinese monks who were not allowed [to stay].

> “... Although I was foolish and ignorant, it is an excellent causal relationship which is not hollow.”

So he really thinks he should take care of this excellent causal relationship, so as not to make it hollow, make it useless. That’s what he says.

> When my late master was giving guidance in Song China, there were those who could study with him and those who could not. Now that my late master, old buddha, has left Song China, it is darker than dark night.

I think for him, his teacher’s death was exactly entire darkness. 

Well, when I was 14, my mother died. But before she died, she had been in bed for several years. So I didn’t feel her presence, because she was always upstairs in bed. But the moment when I heard [of] her death at the school in the morning, immediately I felt the whole world became dark. So I completely lost energy to do anything. I experienced that one. So that’s why at that time I realized how important just the presence of mother was for the children. It’s very important. Even though she didn’t do anything for us, always being in bed, but her presence was very important for us. I realized that at that time. But before that, I didn’t. 

So, that’s why he says, his teacher’s death was a really big loss. A big loss for him, for the monks in China, in Japan too. So he says, “It is darker than dark night.” 

> Why? Because there is no old buddha like my late master—before or after him. Therefore, you who study later should think about this upon hearing these words. Do not think that human and heavenly beings everywhere hear this dharma wheel.

So I think [this] really suggests Dōgen feels a really big loss for the human world and heavenly world, therefore the cosmos, the whole universe, because his teacher was the greatest teacher in Zen Buddhism in those days. That’s why he suggests to us that we should study seriously his words, his life, and through his *teisho*, his dharma words. That’s what he says here. 

He says this here because plum blossoms are connected with dharma transmission. So he tried to say about the dharma transmission, what should we transmit? Of course, *dharma*. But a *dharma* is what? So, through using the term *plum blossom*, which occurs as the design of the paper of the document of dharma transmission. So, that plum blossom is not the usual plum tree; it is really deeply connected with dharma transmission. So that’s why he mentions this. 

#### 18:15

So next, the "plum blossoms in snow.”  Still he is commenting on the plum blossoms, until, I think ... the third line of the second paragraph at page 117. Until here, I think Dōgen comments on the plum blossom.

> The "plum blossoms in snow" is the appearance of an udumbara flower. Although we repeatedly see the eyeball of the true dharma of our Buddha Tathāgata, we do not wink, nor do we smile. Now we correctly transmit and accept that plum blossoms in snow are truly the Tathāgata's eyeball. We take them up and hold them as the eye at the top of the head, as the pupil of the eye.

I think the plum blossoms in snow, plum blossom is very important because it is connected to the dharma transmission, and also biologically, the plum tree is a symbol of strength, blooming in the snow. We can really feel a pure sense of life energies, when you see the scarlet blossoms bloom in the snow. So that’s why plum blossoms are pretty important here. 

So, "plum blossoms in snow" is exactly the same as the *udumbara* flower Shakyamuni Buddha lifted up before his assembly, and then Mahakasyapa smiled. That is the story. So, I think the plum blossom is exactly the same as the udumbara flower, which blooms once every 3,000 years. So, the plum blossom, if you want to receive the key points, the most important points of Buddhist teachings, I think we should really receive plum blossom, or we should really see dynamic movement within everything: trees, birds, skies, all sentient beings. At that time it is called *dharma*. And also if you receive this dharma, it is exactly the same as the plum blossom, or udumbara flower which blooms once every 3,000 years. It’s very rare, but that is why it is very precious. That is what he says here. 

The important point, not only for priests’ life, but also if you live in this world as human beings, whatever kind of occupation you follow, I think the important point is you have to take responsibility for something important you should transmit to the next generation, about human life. In the priest, we say dharma transmission – handed down from generation to generation. 

So, whatever kind of occupation you follow, I think in the human world, there is [be-owned] and [en-own], which human beings handed down from century after century. What it is? This is something we have to transmit to the next generation. 

#### 23:27

> Although we repeatedly see the eyeball of the true dharma of our Buddha Tathāgata, we do not wink, nor do we smile. Now we correctly transmit and accept that plum blossoms in snow are truly the Tathāgata's eyeball. We take them up and hold them as the eye at the top of the head, as the pupil of the eye.

There are two important points. One is, I think the receiving or giving dharma to the next generation is the teaching of oneness, or the teaching of complete unity, complete harmonious state of being where all sentient beings can walk in peace and harmony, hand in hand. So, this is not the dualistic world. That is that through and through, we have to experience this and we have to move towards oneness, the same. You and I are not different – not mixed up, but with our head we understand, we are friends. But still there is a gap; our head doesn’t accept. So until your head completely gives up, surrenders itself to this oneness, I think we should practice it. That is dharma transmission. That is one point.

Second point is, the plum tree you can see on the dharma transmission paper, I think this is exactly the same as the udumbara flower the Buddha Shakyamuni held. This is really true; I mentioned that the truth that we should understand the human world in terms of deep level of time. I think if you go deeply into the realm of time, I think that time is, I mentioned, always there is time, but no time. *No time* and *time* exist together. So that pure sense of time is exactly the same Buddha Shakyamuni saw, and any ancestor saw it. You see this moment, this time; that time you see is exactly the same time the Buddha and ancestor Mahakasyapa saw it. 

That’s why a couple of days ago, I mentioned Mickey Mouse. You can see wherever you chopped time, there is a Mickey Mouse face come up. Mickey Mouse is exactly the same – you see and Buddha see and also Mahakasyapa, Dōgen, Bodhidharma saw. Same thing. From this point, the plum tree you can see is exactly the udumbara flower Shakyamuni Buddha saw. Do you understand? 

That is very important. In order to do this, I think Jōshū Zen Master said the oak tree is Buddha. Why did the Bodhidharma come to China to teach, and then Jōshū Zen Master says “the oak tree in the yard.” But we don’t see the Buddha in the oak tree. If you don’t see it, I think until you can see it, we have to research. It’s very natural, because if you are right in the midst of the wilderness, keep your mind shut, just sit down there – immediately you can feel the same rhythm, of the life of the wilderness, and the rhythm of your life, exactly the same and become one. You can experience that. Last year someone went there, and same things, same experience: that peacefulness, quietness, wonderful wilderness. And 200 years ago, they went there, and the same experience. We experience the same things like that. 

But, there are not many people who try to research the same experience in the human life. That’s why we give up always. Because there are lots of choices, lots of temptations, lots of conflictions, many stuff there. That’s why we don’t want to research. But actually, in the human life there is the same situation as you experience in the wilderness. That’s why if you see the oak tree in the yard, this is exactly the same rhythm there as Buddha saw. That’s why Buddha held up the udumbara flower. It is exactly the same as the oak tree you can see now. Otherwise, you don’t understand this one. 

And also Dōgen Zenji says, if you do *gassho*, this *gassho* is exactly same *gassho* as Buddha did, Dōgen did, and Ancestor did. And then your *gassho* is stable, very stable. That is called *faith*. And also faith is *action*, human action, refined action. 

Is that okay? That is most important. That’s why next he says,

> Now we correctly transmit and accept that plum blossoms in snow are truly the Tathāgata's eyeball. 

Exactly the Tathāgata's skin, muscles, bones and marrow.

> We take them up and hold them as the eye at the top of the head, as the pupil of the eye.

Exactly special eyeballs, the special pupil of eyes. It’s not the usual eye, it’s really Buddha’s eye. 

#### 31:52

So, well, let’s go [to the next paragraph].

> When we enter into plum blossoms and fully study them, there is no room for doubt to arise. They are already the eyeball of “Alone above and below the heavens, I am the honored one,” and again, “most honored in the dharma-world.”

That is what Buddha said when he was born in this world; he said, “Alone above and below the heavens, I am the honored one.” It is not showing up the egoistic sense; that is perfect independence of his presence, in the whole world. I [talked about this] a couple of days ago. In terms of deep understanding of time, you are exactly alone in the whole world. Nobody around. Nobody around to compare, to depend. You don’t believe it, but when you die, you can experience this: you are completely alone in this world. No one helps. Many people around you, but no one helps. People want to help you, but people’s help doesn’t touch. But you can feel people’s kindness and help, so you can accept it. Because people understand we are completely alone in this world. Just like snow; complete snow all over, but your life is just a scarlet plum blossom blooming in the snow. This is your life. 

If you understand this, that is called *dharma transmission*. That is *plum blossom*. That is the symbol of the plum blossom. So in the realm of perfect aloneness, you have to let the flow of your life force bloom, wherever you may be. Then, your life becomes plum blossom, so-called *dharma transmission*. You can see the sublimity of human life. 

So that is what Buddha Shakyamuni says:  “Alone above and below the heavens, I am the honored one,” and again, “most honored in the dharma-world.” So not only the usual cosmos, universe. *Dharma world* means including visible or invisible world. That is called *dharma world*: completely the whole world, entire world. Then, he is the honored one. So from this point, aloneness is *completely* alone. That complete aloneness is not the relative sense, you know? [It’s] absolute aloneness. Then, the plum blossom in snow – in the realm of absolute aloneness. 

Then, that is called *peace*; [great] peace. And also people are very moved by it. That is what he said here. 

#### 36:01

> Therefore, heavenly blossoms in heaven, heavenly blossoms in the human
world, mandara blossoms raining from heaven...

Anyway, he mentions the plum blossoms. Plum blossom means, letting the flower of your life force bloom in the realm of complete aloneness, so-called *snow all over*. At that time, that is that your life is really blooming, alive. That life is exactly the same as the life of trees, the life of rains, the life of thunderstorms, the life of snows, and many things. That’s why here it says, many blossoms, heavenly blossoms, human blossoms. You know, human blossoms there. And the blossoms raining from the heavens when the Buddha was born, and many kinds of rains fell from the heavens, it says: 

> great mandara blossoms, manjushaka blossoms, great manjushaka blossoms, and all blossoms of inexhaustible lands in the ten directions are one family of plum blossoms
in snow. 

So, completely all over, all over the land. In ten directions, everywhere, this is just plum blossoms blooming. It means, if you really realize how lonely, how alone you are in this world, and then thoroughly you go deeply into this aloneness, then you can see completely nothing to depend on, so-called *emptiness*. But that emptiness is not something pessimistic, it’s kind of a new energy you can get. So you can stand up there. That is really pure energy of life – like a scarlet plum blossom blooming in the snow. 

So, *all over*, everything exists like this. That’s what he says here; “inexhaustible lands in the ten directions are one family of plum blossoms in snow.” *I* am here, and *I* let the flower of my life-force bloom right now. It is one family of plum blossoms, among the many, many plum blossoms there. *Many, many plum blossoms* means completely everything exists like plum blossoms: scarlet flowers blooming in the snow, all over. That is everything. Among everything’s life, and then *I* exist. That is one family of plum blossoms, he says. This is one Buddha appears here. You sit here, one plum blossom Buddha exists. 

So,

> Because they bloom through the virtue of plum blossoms, ...

This is the virtue of plum blossoms. The plum blossoms have invisible energies, virtues, to help himself, herself and also others, in the realm of *snow all over*. 

> ... billions of blossoms are one family of plum blossoms. 

So everything is the same, exactly. The trees, birds, you, everything is exactly the same as plum blossoms in snow, all over. 

> They should be called young plum blossoms. Furthermore, flowers in the sky, flowers on the earth, and flowers of samadhi are all the large and small members of plum blossoms'
family.

So, small blossom, large blossom. This is small Katagiri, small blossom blooming here. But this is nothing but one small blossom among many many blossoms there. 

So, many plum flowers are always blooming everywhere. 

#### 41:30

> To form billions of lands within blossoms and to bloom in the land is the work of plum blossoms. 

I think they [missed] a few words before “the work of plum blossoms”: *due to* the work of plum blossoms.

> To form billions of lands within blossoms and to bloom in the land is due to the work of plum blossoms. 

Thanks to the virtue of plum blossoms, we can see many beings, myriad, myriad things in this world: suffering, pleasures, consciousness, from century after century. So you can see many lands

> Without the virtue of plum blossoms there is no virtue of rain or dew. 

So without virtue of plum blossoms, it is impossible to have rain and dew, to have virtue of rain and dew, because rain and dew are also the same state of existence as plum blossoms. Blooming scarlet flowers in the snow all over: this is the rain, this is the dew. That’s why rain has its own virtue: to make everything wet and to grow. 

> Life streams issue from plum blossoms. 

That’s why life streams are not only life streams of human beings, but also life stream of whole universe. The whole universe issues from plum blossoms. That [means] that plum blossoms is the very pure sense of the momentum of energy, blooming in the snow all over. 

That is called *dharma transmission*. We have to receive it, we have to transmit this dharma from century after century. This is dharma transmission. So he talks about dharma transmission through the plum blossoms. 

Do you have some questions? 

#### 44:29

**Question:** Roshi? 

**Katagiri Roshi:** *Hai.*

**Question:** When Buddha said, “Alone above and below the heavens, I am the honored one” at birth: if that recognition was there at birth, was he enlightened then? And if so, why did he have to go through all that he had to go through to become enlightened? 

**Katagiri Roshi:** Uh, excuse me?

**Question:** When Buddha said, “Alone above and below,” at birth, was he enlightened then? 

**Katagiri Roshi:** I think actually his birth is already enlightened. 

**Question:** Mm-hmm. Why did he have to go through all that he went through? 

**Katagiri Roshi:** Oh, because if you don’t practice it, you know, such enlightenment doesn’t work in everyday life. So I think we have to *ignite*... start the engine. Even though you have a wonderful car, if you don’t have ignition, you know, it’s pretty hard to... Ignition itself is everyday life, human life. You have to anyway start it. That is work. So, whole stuff working together simultaneously. 

You know, if you understand something, the idea of what you have understanding exists here, and another idea of understanding is here, and here. Always separated. Separately always many ideas in the head. But they don’t connect with life. 

Sometimes you can say, “Oh, right! That one thing comes up and is connected to everyday life.” Then you say, “Well, I understand it.” It’s really feel. 

So you have lots of ideas already. But when you were born, anyway, completely pure. You have ideas, already ideas, but ideas are completely pure. Just calm. That is called Dharmakaya Buddha. So when you were born, you were Dhammakaya Buddha. *Hoshi* means really pure sense of truth. 

And then you are educated at the school, and you can learn a lot and practice it, and then you can have Nirmanakaya Buddha. Nirmanakaya Buddha. 

So *due to the virtue of plum blossoms* means due to the virtue of Dharmakaya Buddha, you never grow, you never mature in your life. So you can mature means already you are given by the virtue of Dharmakaya Buddha, that means the truth. And then, you can really educate yourself, and you can see the maturity of personality, and then you can know how to live with the people. That is Sambhogakaya Buddha. 

Sambhogakaya Buddha means you are human, but not human, you are Buddha. Manifestion of the Dharmakaya means the whole universe. So it’s actually working. 

**Question:** It seems like it works backwards. 

**Katagiri Roshi:** Backwards? 

**Question:** Well, at birth you have the Dharmakaya; at birth you have truth. 

**Katagiri Roshi:** Mm-hmm. 

**Question:** And then you work backwards until you finally realize what you had when you started out with it. 

**Katagiri Roshi:** Well, yes; in a sense. 

The *Saddharma Pundarika Sutra* says you have to come back to the source, the beginning of the birth. Because you remember; you remember many things, but it’s very pure. So why does the *Saddharma Pundarika Sutra* always have to talk about Buddha's teaching? Because they want to guide, they want to take you to the source of life. That is the birth; the very beginning of the birth. 

#### 49:51

**Question:** Roshi? I got a little confused when you were saying those things. I heard Dharmakaya Buddha at birth, and truth. And then you said Nirmanakaya Buddha and Sambhogakaya, and I don't know those words. 

**Katagiri Roshi:** Nirmanakaya Buddha means the Buddha who has a human body. The human beings, anyway; Nirmanakaya. Sambhogakaya is the Dharmakaya Buddha is actualized within within the human body. And that is Sambhogakaya. 

**Questioner:** So you said that after education and things you can be a Nirmalakaya Buddha. 

**Katagiri Roshi:** Yes, Nirmanakaya.

**Questioner:** And you said by the virtue of Dharmakaya you become Sambhogakaya.

**Katagiri Roshi:** Yes. Well, due to the virtue of Sambhogakaya and also Nirmalakaya you can become Sambhogakaya. 

#### 51:20

**Question:** When you speak about plum blossoms and the Buddha, in my western mind I think what that means is that the plum blossoms *contain* the Buddha, and Katagiri Roshi contains the Buddha. But I think that's wrong. I don't know how to think right. 

**Katagiri:** *[Laughs.]* Yes, *contain*. If you try to talk in words, I think we can say “contain.” But Dōgen Zen says it's not *contain*, it's not *possess*, it's not *have*; *is*. *You are Buddha*. Dōgen Zen uses this kind of expression: *is*. *Is* means no gap between. *I am Buddha.* 

**Questioner:** I am Phillip, too.

**Katagiri:** Yeah. Sure. 

Well, intellectually we understand it, but the practice is very hard. 

**Questioner:** Yes.

**Katagiri:** Because I cannot easily take care of myself as a Buddha; always Katagiri comes up first. *[He laughs.]* So [we are] very confused. That's why confused life comes up. But right in the middle of confusion, still we have to continue to practice it as a Buddha. We have to take care of, deal with ourselves as a Buddha, as a Buddha, constantly. Then, finally, Buddha and I become *[hand clap]*. That is called *Sambhogakaya Buddha*.
