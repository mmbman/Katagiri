## October 7, 1988 Dharma Talk by Dainin Katagiri Roshi

#### 0:00

**Katagiri Roshi:** [I will explain what] Dōgen Zenji tried to say in the chapter “The Flowers in the Sky,” because this is the last talk. 

In this chapter, I think Dōgen Zenji tries to teach us that we should accept, we should see, we should understand and accept everything in the world as a Buddha – including enlightenment, delusions, and the hells, and the hungry ghosts. Whatever they are, all things in the human world must be Buddha. Because this is a statement mentioned by Shakyamuni Buddha twenty-five hundred years ago. Without this understanding, without this acceptance, I think the Buddhist teaching doesn’t make sense. Whatever kind of Buddhist denomination – Pure Land School mentions, particularly Shinran in Japan, the founder of the Pure Land School, emphasized strongly the “sin” of human beings. This seems to be similar with the Christian sense, but I don’t think it is the same. Quite different, because even this understanding should be based on the truth that all beings in the world must be Buddha. Then, we can see, we can accept this teaching, we can understand this teaching as Buddhism. 

So, I think that’s why Dōgen Zenji tries to teach us this truth, based on the Shakyamuni Buddha’s enlightenment: “I and all sentient beings are enlightened simultaneously.” 

I think the same applies to the meaning of the Precepts. If you want to practice the Precepts, I think we should stand up in this truth first. This is a *basic* attitude towards human world, human beings. Otherwise, you cannot deal with the human world filled with ugly and miserable state of human life. 

So that’s why this is our practice. Particularly in the monastery. We practice basic manner, basic training in the monastery, to learn to penetrate this truth as a Buddha. That’s why we do *gassho*, and chant the verse when you use the toilet – whatever it is. And then, if you continue to practice this basic training, basic manner of this truth, then you can put this into everyday living. Otherwise, it is very difficult to deal with everyday life. 

So, from this point, one point [of] Dōgen Zenji’s teaching is what we do as a practice is not the practice separated from enlightenment, because we practice in the realm of Buddha, Buddha’s world. So, *you* do, but if you see your practice with your naked eye, then it is called *practice*. In terms of Buddha’s eye, we call it *enlightenment*. But this is dualistic, because Buddha’s eyes [and] our naked eyes are not different. So first of all, we have to stand up in the realm of Buddha’s world: this is basic. And then practice, and thoughts coming up, and delusion coming up, and enlightenment coming up: all are nothing but activities in the Buddha’s world. 

But, when you see, when you face activities appearing in the Buddha’s world, they are different. So, tree is tree, tree is not same as pebble. So, pebble is pebble, tree is tree. So, we have to deal with the tree as a tree, because tree is a being blooming in the Buddha’s world. The same applies to pebbles, insects, and all sentient beings. 

Human beings always have a certain custom of comparing and conflicting with our knowledge immediately. And then finally we are usually at a loss how to deal with human life, the concrete aspect of human life. Even though intellectually we understand, we try to stand up in the Buddha’s world, [still] emotionally we don’t. And finally, we are going far from the basis. 

So that’s why we have to learn this teaching, this truth, from many angles, again and again. We have to listen to this truth again and again, *forever*. Then, we can practice *a little bit*, in your everyday life. 

Someone complains that we are always teaching, but the students don’t practice, don’t actualize Buddha’s teaching in their life, because fighting, politics, always fighting in many senses. So why do you practice in the Zen center? Something like that. And then someone complained to Zen Center, because before they came into Zen Center, they thought Zen Center must be beautiful and pure because that is a religious place. But when he or she got into this Zen Center, they can see the many, many ugly things. Desire, selfish, rough language, rough manner, fighting; in many ways. So, finally I don’t like Zen Center. Always they complain like this. 

At that time, wherever they may go – there is nowhere, nowhere they thought, so-called pure place, religious place. 

So, I think the... Where am I? [...] Yes, that’s right. 

[...] There are three kinds of practice, mainly. Listening to Buddha’s teaching; meditating [on] what you have listened; and practice, actualization. Three. Three points are very important. 

So, practice, and contemplate or meditate or thinking deeply [on] Buddha’s teaching, and listen to Buddha’s teaching again and again. 

So, Dōgen Zenji says in The Record of Things Heard, Dōgen Zenji says, “Listen to Buddha’s teaching again and again, forever, like walking in the mist.” Your dress get wet, before you know. That kind of thing. 

So, we have to continue to listen, and continue to practice, continue to contemplate, meditate. Meditate means thinking deeply. If you think deeply, it turns into practice, actual practice. So, very naturally you can get into the entrance of actualization, practice. 

So you have to continue to listen to the Buddha’s teaching. Then, it penetrates your skin, muscle, bone, and marrow. 

We always talk about the Buddha’s compassion and wisdom, kindness, et cetera – but how many people can practice actualized compassion in this world? 

Look at yourself. How many years you practiced Buddha’s teaching, and practiced [being a] lay Buddhist or a priest?

I was very surprised to see the Zen Center in San Francisco when something happened there. Very surprised. It was a great lesson for me, because *no one* expressed compassion, *real* compassion. In the middle of the whirl, everyone’s screaming and fighting and expressing their desires. Just like hell. Including [priests]. Before that I didn’t notice who I was, because I always teach compassion and wisdom: be kind. But when I see that situation, I got a big shock, and it was a really big lesson for me. 

So immediately I told them, “Please be calm, and don’t express all over the world, A person did this, B person did this. So be quiet, and then one by one you should deal with some trouble.” But from the beginning, there was no space for people who can take a deep breath peacefully. 

So I felt really sorry for me – because it was not Zen Center, it was me. 

I think we should recognize this one, how much we can practice Buddha’s teaching day to day. In other words, precepts: do not be angry. How often can you be calm? It’s very difficult to obey even the one precept. Not angry: everyone knows very much how much the anger and the feeling of anger hurt people. But when we face it, we don’t know, we are completely at loss what to do. Finally, we fight. 

So I think standing up in the Buddhist world is not so simple. Not so simple. That’s why this *basic* practice is very important. 

You don’t pay attention to this. You know, when I was practicing [at Eiheiji], Hashimoto Roshi always pointed out [to] follow that teaching. Not difficult things he mentioned; very simple concrete aspects. For instance, when I make a tea for the guest, matcha tea, then I clean the cup and put it on the floor. Immediately he said it was not the place for the cleaning cloth. Put it on a tray; there is a certain place for the wiping cloth. So immediately I put it there. This is a very good suggestion, and he took me to basic practice. When I do *gassho*, I always made a space between both hands. He said, “Don’t make a space between!” Well, I didn’t understand this. But it’s not the main point, that you should have a right gassho without any space – that is not the point. Just do; just follow his suggestion and then just do it. Always we do basic practice, basic practice; standing in the Buddha’s world. Do you understand this? 

But always immediately our knowledge starts to spin. “What do you mean by this?” Immediately we explain, and complain, et cetera. But I think that practice is very important for us. 

That’s why in Sōtō Zen, particularly Dōgen Zen mentions *menmitsu gyōji* (綿密行持). *Gyōji* (行持) means “continuous practice.” *Menmitsu* (綿密) means... how can I say, it’s very difficult to translate it. Not “meticulously,” but very precise. *Precise*, very precise. Precisely you have to hit the head, otherwise you cannot get a point in *kendo*, you know, sports. Or if you want to close the pen, the lid must be placed in the proper place. Precise, it’s very precise. If you even slightly, a little bit go away, you cannot do it. 

So mental practices also, it is very difficult to do this, because always consciousness complains, analyzes, synthesizes. Body is simple: *Do it*. “Yes, sir.” You can do it. But if you have very analytical, critical mind, then immediately you don’t do it. 

So that is *menmitsu gyōji*: continuous practice, in precision. 

“Precision” means not *cold* precision, okay? It’s *precise*, but it’s *warm*, very warm. Kind, compassionate. 

I think if you practice you will understand this. And people feel this; people feel this. It’s not *rigid*; from the rigid form I think we can feel kind of cold feeling, you know? But *menmitsu gyōji*, continuous practice in precision, is not cold; you can feel warmth, human warmness and compassion there. Nevertheless there is a wisdom called *precision*; very precise. Cutting off any thread of a thought, and then just do it. 

That is *menmitsu gyōji*. So I think this is what we do, even for two months. This is a very basic practice. 

Just like, I told you the day before yesterday [about the] practice of archery. I think during the practicing [archery], I think your target is always far from you, and settle down there, set up. It’s not moving; it’s set up. And then you have to stand up: you have to master the form, how to hold [the bow], and you have to learn your consciousness when you stand up straightly how it is. You have to learn. Then, precisely you have to stand up, day to day. Then you can hit the target which is fixed. Okay? Fixed target there. This is basic. 

So when we do practice in the monastery, always we are hitting a fixed target. Okay? Remember this. And then, after this, maybe you can shoot something moving, a moving target. It’s [no longer] necessary to [have a] fixed target. Because this is daily life; in daily life always your target is moving. Do you understand this? 

So when you see anger, when you see compassion, you don’t see, you don’t know what the compassion is in everyday life, you don’t know anger, you don’t know the kindness in everyday life. But when you see the kindness as a fixed target, you can understand it. This is the teaching, this is the suggestions; this is a fixed target. But when you go out of this, you have to share your life and then each other’s [lives], then you can learn what the kindness is. Much more deeply, you can learn the feeling of a kindness coming up. So then, when you go back to everyday life, if you practice this basic, I think if you have basic practice, day to day, then it is really alive in everyday life, you can apply to everyday life. 

So that’s why *monshishu* – listening and contemplating and practice – must be continuous; forever. 

I think why we have to mention this. We must be first standing in the Buddhist world, as *nondualism*, we say. Because if you don’t stand up there, you cannot deal with everyday life with compassion, kindness; soft, gentle. You cannot be gentle, considerate, and charitable to everything in your everyday life. You cannot be. Because you fall into the dualism immediately. In the dualism, we are very easily tied up with criticism: good or bad, right or wrong. And then like or dislike, that emotional feeling coming up. So thinking, judgement, evaluation, and emotional affliction coming up: like or dislike. Then, constantly snowball is rolling down to the foot of the mountain. Finally we are completely at a loss. We call this *samsara*. 

That’s why, we understand, we are living in the dualism, and everyone recognizes this; no one disputes the fact that we are living in dualism. Yes, [we are]. But – there is something we have to do. In order to deal with the dualism with compassion, with kindness, with wisdom – you can be gentle, considerate, and charitable. 

I always remember the Buddhist story of Angulimala. Do you know that story? One of the Buddha’s disciples. He used to be a robber; but not the usual robber or thief, because not only they are robbing something from others, but also taking peoples’ life. So he was really cruel robber and thief. Angulimala killed people, I think almost a thousand people. And then finally he watched for the opportunity to attack the Buddha Shakyamuni, but he couldn’t do it. Finally, he gave up. And then later he became a monk, he became Buddha’s disciple. And then I think his practice was very strong and stable, and Buddhist scripture mentions that he attained Arahat. 

Then one day he went to practice *takuhatsu* – begging – in the village, in the town. There were many people who had enmity against him, and whenever he went out of his temple or his room, those people threw rocks and stones or sticks at him. Then finally he came back with lots of blood and shabby, broken *koromo* and robes, and he was completely exhausted and despairing. And then Buddha saw him and said, said, “You should receive it with patience.” He repeats two [times]: “You should receive it with patience.” “You should receive it with patience.” And then he mentions that, as a matter of fact, you will have to have the suffering in the hell for hundreds and thousands of millions of years, because you did this and that. But now you get, instead of being in the hell and receiving the suffering in the hell, you receive now that suffering. Shakyamuni Buddha mentioned like this. What I want to tell you is, receive it with patience. This is not so easy. 

Receive and with the patience, in Sanskrit is called *adhivāseti*. Adhivāseti means “to consent.” To consent, be patient, and receive, and to wait. Adivasti has four meanings there. 

To consent: to consent means that you should be agreed with the presence of your life, presence of the human world. Agree, beyond, good or bad, right or wrong, anyway you should consent. And then, that means you can accept it. 

I mention always holding a bunch of entangled thread. Entangled thread is kind of human relation, created by many conditions, cause and effect, and many conditions. So it’s not so simple; human life is kind of a bunch of tangled thread. But I don’t think you can cut off immediately with short temper, because many conditions are very important, because this is the truth, that we have been grown up right in the middle of conditions, being supported by many beings: many friends, and teachers, and trees, birds, nature, all things. This is called *the conditions*: warmth, and the rain, the cold, many things. So I think we should, as best as we can, we should use carefully trees, birds, those conditions. That is you cannot cut with a scissors immediately with a short-temper. That’s why you have to hold it gently. So *hold it* means to consent; to accept the world. And then, you can receive; that is called receive. And then, next, you can be patient. If you receive, and then be patient, then you should work on untangling, one by one. One by one, instead of immediately cutting the thread with the scissors. 

Then, that is patience. And sometimes, if you get the one thread, simultaneously, two or three or four threads are following, and all of a sudden, you can find the untangled thread, by pulling just one. Have you ever experienced this before? *[He laughs.]* I did. 

So, this is the patience. Particularly when you are in difficulties, you don’t practice this. So I think you should do this, be patient. 

And then, *wait*. *Wait* is not to wait for nothing; *wait* means work on something, and then wait for the time to be free from those untangled threads and all things. 

So those four are called *receive with patience*. 

So, that is called *dealing with your everyday life with compassion*. 

The analogy I said is probably pretty good for you. If you don’t understand the dealing with everyday life with compassion, I think you should remember this analogy. And also the meaning of the *adhivāseti*: patience, and receive. Listen to this meaning, listen to the analogy, and then you should practice it day to day. That is called *standing in the Buddha’s world*. Because there is no continuous feeling of hatred or certain strong emotions. Of course emotion are coming up, but they’re dissipated as soon as possible, if you stand up in the Buddha’s world again and again. 

So, that is our practice. In order to deal with everyday life like this, I think we need a particular practice. That is the basic practice we do in the monastery. 

So, Dōgen Zenji [talks about] that point in the chapter “Flowers in the Sky.” 

Do you have a question? 

#### 37:51

**Question:** *[Mostly inaudible.]*

**Katagiri Roshi:** Mm-hmm. I think so. Pianist, and organist, and dancers; they practice basic practice and their practice is really precise. Otherwise you cannot master anything... [much less] the spiritual world.

#### 39:30

**Question:** It seems though, on what you mentioned about everyday living, and standing up every day in Buddha’s world, that you should really do that in every situation, every relationship you have. When another person has a problem, it’s your problem, because it’s the same character. 

**Katagiri Roshi:** Yes, that’s true. 

**Questioner:** *[Inaudible.]*

**Katagiri Roshi:** If you accept the other’s problem like that, I think you can stop criticism toward others. 

**Questioner:** Yes. When I was short of patience, I remember experiencing that when someone made a mistake, I criticized them, and then I made the same mistake the next day. And I realized that any mistake I saw made, I would make. 

**Katagiri Roshi:** Right. 

#### 40:38

**Question:** I don’t know if this [makes sense]. If you go real deeply into suffering – not just conscious suffering or unconscious suffering, I mean very deeply when you feel it – it seems to turn into bliss. They’re sort of inseperable. Does that make any sense?

**Katagiri Roshi:** Yeah, it makes sense.

#### 41:03

**Question:** What do you mean by *accept*?

**Katagiri Roshi:** “Accept”? Consent. 

**Questioner:** You say “consent”... then does that mean that...

**Katagiri Roshi:** Do you understand *consent to your presence*? Your presence. 

**Questioner:** To *my* presence?

**Katagiri Roshi:** Yeah.

**Questioner:** I don’t understand ...

**Katagiri Roshi:** *Agree* with your presence. That means *admit* the presence of your existence. That is consent. 

**Questioner:** *[unintelligible]*

**Katagiri Roshi:** Yeah. Before you think of it. 

It doesn’t make sense? 

*[Laughter.]*

**Someone:** Is that the same as to become one with?

**Katagiri Roshi:** Yes...

**Questioner:** To become one with *my* existence.

**Katagiri Roshi:** Well you asked me, what is accept? The meaning of accept. That’s why accept is not accept something [like] your object. 

**Questioner:** Oh, not accept conditions *out there*?

**Katagiri Roshi:** No. 

**Questioner:** Accept *me*?

**Katagiri Roshi:** *You*. Your presence. 

**Questioner:** Oh.

**Katagiri Roshi:** And then, simultaneously, all the world is coming in. 

Accept Angulimala’s existence. 

**Questioner:** Here.

**Katagiri Roshi:** Yes. That’s it. That is most important. 

And then many stories coming up from Angulimala’s life. He used to be a thief, and he killed thousands of people: many flowers coming up. But those things are not some objects to criticize, because they are nothing but the flowers in the sky in the Buddha’s world. So we have to *receive it*, *accept it*, and then work on it, one by one. That’s why Buddha said you should receive and be patient: *adhivāseti*. 

Do you understand? Is that okay?
