### Transcript

#### 0:00

**Katagiri Roshi:**

> 5.
>
> You should always inform the director of the hall where you are going to be, day or night. Do not play around according to your own impulses; your actions affect the discipline of the entire assembly. No one can tell if this moment is not the end of this life. It would be truly regrettable to die while indulging in pleasures.
>
> – From “Regulations for the Auxiliary Cloud Hall (Jūundō-shiki)” in *Moon in a Dewdrop*, edited by Kazuaki Tanahashi.

> You should always inform the director of the hall where you are going to be, day or night.

This is strict rules in the monastery. Whatever reason you have to go out, you have to go out with the Ino's permission. In this case, “the director of the hall” means *Ino*. The Ino is a kind of, what would you say, kind of the “Minister of Justice.” *[He chuckles, and a few people laugh.]* Minister of justice. So if you do something wrong, the Ino will pick you up and let you out. This is Inos. So the Ino has to take a big responsibility for taking care of monastic regulations and also monks' everyday life. So that's why for whatever reason you have, when you go out you should get permission from him. So “you should always inform the director of the hall where you are going to be day or night.”

> Do not play around according to your own impulses.

When I was at Eiheiji monastery, we had a *big* earthquake. Thousands of people died. That was the day before the memorial day of Dogen's death, I think [June] 28th. I forget the exact year, but maybe 1948 or 9. (*Transcriber’s Note:* The 1948 Fukui earthquake killed over three thousand people.)

Anyway, a friend of mine went out to Fukui City. I don't know why, but anyway, he got the permission and he went to Fukui City. I think it took about an hour and a half by train. [It was a] very slow train; now it's pretty good, but in those days, the train was very slow. So he went. The earthquake [was] between 4 and 5 [PM];  I forget exactly. He thought in Fukui City he wanted to watch a movie that day. So he finished the business and then he went to the movie theater. Right before he tried to buy the ticket, he [stopped], because he thought, “Tonight we have our memorial service of Dogen's death, so I should go home, go back to Eiheiji monastery.” So he [stopped trying] to buy a ticket, and left the theater. A couple blocks away, he still wanted to watch the movie, so he came back again and tried to buy a ticket, but again he [didn’t]. *[He laughs.]* And then, fortunately, he didn't watch the movie. He left Fukui City, and he was on the way back to Eiheiji monastery on the train. And then the earthquake happened. And the train turned over. *[He laughs.]* The railroad was completely bumpy, and huge earthquake; the road cracked, and then the train complete turned over. He fainted. *[He laughs.]* Later, anyway, he came back to Eiheiji monastery. 

At that time, in the earthquake, the people who watched the movie in the theater all died. Because in the movie theater there is was no pole, you know, big [room], but no particular pole there [holding up the roof]. So if you have an earthquake, particularly that earthquake is showed as shaking the earth right and left like this, and then immediately like this. So that's a very dangerous earthquake. [...] And then [...] all the houses crashed, and then the theater completely crashed. All the people died. 

So he survived, anyway. He survived. He fainted, but he survived. 

So, I think, that's why: “Do not play around according to your own impulses.”

*[Big laughter.]*

According to your own impulses, anyway. So Dogen Zenji says if this is right. *[He laughs.]* 

Why we have to get permission from Ino – there are two reasons. One reason is that:

> Your actions affect the discipline of the entire assembly.

This is one reason. Because, I told you before, practicing in the monastery is going in smooth succession, with the people together. So whatever you do, very naturally your actions affect everyone. So, if you don't obey the regulation, very naturally your actions affect the discipline of the entire assembly. This is one reason. 

The second reason is:

> No one can tell if this moment is not the end of this life. 

You should remember this one. 

My teacher always said, when I go out, when I travel, my teacher said to me always, you should... well, let me say it like this. He said, “You should clean your room, you should put your things in, well, not exactly, but in order, neatly. Whenever you die, no problem. So as not to create a problem to the people, anyway, because you *might* die when you go out. So, maybe this, when you go out from this temple, this is maybe last to see you, and to see your people. So, that's why you should clean your room, you should put your clothes and books and whatever they are, anyway, you should [put] everything neatly in order, and then bow, prostrate three times to the Buddha, and to the teacher, say goodbye. In Japanese, [*ittekimasu*]. Goodbye, in English, you say goodbye. So, three bows, and then, you say goodbye. 

This is very important; this is a priest attitude to everyday life. This is a practice, a practice of impermanence, every day. Wherever you may go, this is a last. 

So, that's why, that opportunity is very important. When you see like this, this is maybe last. 

I think when you have a wedding service, and then maybe wedding or marriage is first trip, you know. To learn what's human life, and creating, planting into new life. Of course. But, maybe last. Maybe last. [...]

In San Francisco, a British woman and a British gentleman were married by Suzuki Roshi and I. And they went then to their honeymoon. And on the freeway, both of them died in a traffic accident. 

So, I don't know what will happen; no one knows. So, that is really impermanence. Our life is just like a dew drop. 

So, that's why, my teacher tells me, like this. It is a great teaching for me. That is, it is not important to bow three times, or to put everything in neat order, after you leave your room. It is not important; the important point is to practice impermanence, to realize impermanence through that practice. Behind that practice, there is very profound teaching you can taste, you can digest. 

So the practice of impermanence is not to think, or to disparage, it is not [...] the impermanent. Anyway you have to do it, quietly, whatever you think, you have to do it.

So that's why, Dogen Zenji says you should get permission from the Ino and go out, because this is last. 

That's why he says, “No one can tell if this moment is not the end of this life.”

> It would be truly regrettable to die while indulging in pleasures.

Of course. 

So, that's why, when Hashimoto Roshi when he was at the monastery, I learned a lot from him, because I traveled with him many times, and his life was exactly practicing impermanence, just like this. So, every moment is the last moment. And he was always sitting straightly, just like zazen. And he didn't talk so much. When it was necessary, he talked, but he didn't talk so much, just sat down. When he slept, he just slept. And his sitting was straight. I was very surprised. So, I tried to imitate him, his sitting, and I couldn't last for 30 minutes, because it's too hard. In zazen, you can do everyone sitting for 40 minutes, but in the train, everyone chatting, and relaxed, and then only you just sit like this. *[He demonstrates, and there is laughter.]* It's funny; people feel funny. [...] But I tried to emulate him, but I didn't last even for 30 minutes. So, next moment: “Oh, forget it.” *[He laughs.]* 

And also, he always carried three kinds of robe, miniature robe. He made three kinds of miniature robe. Have you ever seen that? Very small, tiny, five rakusu, and seven *jo* okesa, and nine *jo* robe. And always he carried them. It's not a kind of mysterious protector, you know. It's not kind of mysterious deity to protect you. But his attitude, [...] his spirit was that he was always living with the Buddha. Because when the Buddha was alive, he lived [with] three robes and one bowl; that's it. So three robes and one bowl. But in Japan we don't wear the three robes always. So, when we go out, only a rakusu, so five *jos*. So, he always carried, that's why he made small miniature robes, three, five, seven, nine jo of robes, and carried them. That means he was always living with the Buddha. [...] Because no one can tell if this moment is last or beginning. No. He really practiced the Buddha’s teaching, anyway. 

So, that's why Dogen Zenji says, you should get the permission. 

Maybe I told you, at Tassajara, one of the students went shopping in Monterey and disappeared; he didn't came back. But anyway, before they left they got the permission from the director of Tassajara, and then they went shopping. But on the way, one person didn't come back. And then his partner didn't know where he was. *[He laughs.]* So, finally, we were very concerned about him; at midnight all students looked for him in the mountains, but we couldn't find him. The next day, he came back. And then we asked him, "Where were you?" So, he said, "I stayed on the Monterey." And then, at that time Tatsugami Roshi was there, and Tatsugami Roshi asked him, "You should apologize to everyone, this morning after breakfast." He asked him to apologize, walking around the zendo, according to the monastic way. And then he did it, he did it. But, right after that, he hid it, and then he left. *[He laughs.]* Do you understand? 

He didn't understand this meaning. I think, if you get permission from the ino and then go out, that [...] regulation. One reason is, wherever you may go, you cannot live alone. So, your action affects everyone, wherever you may go. Because your action creates the world. If you become violent, the whole world is violent. Your violent action will affect everyone. So you should not live alone, wherever you may go. This is one reason. Because the reason is this, no one knows that this moment is last or beginning. So, all you do is, do your best to live. Helping others. Not to bother people, anyway. Because others also are in the same situation. That's why we have to obey this regulation. But, you should understand the *spirit* of the regulation. In other words, *behind* the regulation. What does he mean? *Why* do we have to obey? That's why Dogen talks about this. This is very kind suggestions for us. 

So, monastic rule is not an order, given [by] a boss, given [by] an Ino, or anybody. You should understand how human life, how the human world are going. You should understand. In order to live in peace and harmony, in the [impermanence] of the human world, human beings, we have to follow the regulations, to the minimum. And then, you can live in peace and harmony. This is a monastic rule. If you don't understand, you don't want to obey regulations. 

#### 24:15

Next:

> 6. 
> 
> Do not be concerned with the faults of other persons. Do not see others' faults with a hateful mind. There is an old saying that if you stop seeing others' faults, then naturally you are respected by seniors and revered by juniors. Do not imitate others' faults; just cultivate virtue. Buddha prohibited wrongdoing, but didn’t say to hate wrongdoing. 
>
> – From “Regulations for the Auxiliary Cloud Hall (Jūundō-shiki)” in *Moon in a Dewdrop*, edited by Kazuaki Tanahashi; modified by Katagiri Roshi.

This [should also be] considered again and again in your life. 

The monastic rule is not the regulation for only monks. [It is] adapted to everyone. 

So, “do not be concerned with the faults of other persons.” That means do not get into, do not participate in it. Don't be involved in it. 

For instance, I give suggestions very often, nevertheless, still you repeat the same things. Blowing your nose, yawning, or sighing, or coughing, without so much noise. Reduce the sound, make the sound of blowing nose and thus, well, smaller, anyway. But still you do it. Just imagine, if I ask you, "Don’t blow your nose recklessly." Maybe your mind will say, "Well, it is not right." You know pretty well, even though you feel pleasure and satisfied, but it’s not right exactly. 

So, anyway, ten categories there. So, all you have to do is, anyway, what kind of grasses do you want to drop your dew? That is not a matter of discussion; you have to make a choice, you have to make a decision and do it. Do something with a sincere heart, anyway. With all sentient beings. That is a way to manifest yourself as a Buddha. Behave as a Buddha, anyway. 

So very naturally, not kill, or not speaking of violence, et cetera, the 16 precepts are there. But this is not moral regulations, or... Anyway, you should handle the precepts with really deep compassion. Compassion is not to deal with a precept with hatred, or love. And you should deal with all sentient beings with equanimity, with compassion. With the same attitude. Stand up. So you become magnanimous. And then, you deal with evil as it is, you deal with the good as it is, because your discriminating mind still operates, so you cannot ignore, so you judge, you discriminate between good and evil. But don't handle evil with hatred, et cetera. Hatred, any emotion, put it aside. You have to stand up with white paper; completely [blank] nature of spirit. Just like a white paper. And then you should understand what's the difference, because differentiation is in the human world. 

So, handle the evils as it is. That is your attitude; your attitude, your actions, what you have to do from moment to moment. 

That's why this moment, what kind of, how do you deal with your life? 

Because moment is moment, life in the moment, from moment to moment, is going in the time process, so your discriminating mind is working quickly. So you have to discriminate between good and evil. And then all you have to do is, without creating hatred, anyway, deal with the evil as it is. That means, do you want to drop your dew on the evil? Well, then immediately your life [is in an] evil world. 

This is really compassion. And next moment, still you can have a chance to drop your dew onto what kind of grasses? Buddha? Bodhisattva? This moment you can become Buddha, but next moment you can be evil, hell. So from moment to moment, anyway, you should do it. So don't miss the chance. Without missing the chance, as best as you can, you try to get the chance to manifest yourself as a Buddha. That's all you can do. Before you create a passion of hatred or love, et cetera. 

This is a precept; Buddhist precept. 

I see sometimes a person who is *very* strict in handling the evil opposed to good. So completely hate the evils. Have you ever seen such a person in your life? Evil is *completely* evil. Very strictly, very rigidly. No space to listen to the voice of evil; no way. It's fine, it's fine, but remember: if you [are] like this, you will get sooner or later revenge. Do you understand? Revenge, because you are too rigid. 

You know, if you bump your body against the wall with 100% power – what kind of reaction comes up? It's very clear, isn't it? *[Laughter.]* But if you don't bump, what kind of reaction comes? Nothing. It is not so good, too. So how do you bump bump your body against the wall? 100% power? It seems to be clear, and wonderful and brave. I don’t think so. Who hurts? You hurt. 

So, I think that's why very naturally people don't accept you; people don't accept such a person. Even though temporarily it’s worse, but it doesn't last for long. So very naturally, he himself will be, what would you say, suffocated to death, right in the middle of hatred of evil. 

So that's why Dogen Zenji says like this. So, “Do not see other sports with hateful mind.”

*[Tape change.]*

And also, the original text says:

> There is an old sayign that if you stop seeing others’ faults and your virtue, then naturally you are respected by seniors and are revered by juniors. 

I mean, you can be respected by everyone. This is also very important for us too. 

Maybe a couple of days ago, maybe yesterday, in order to emphasize strongly the meaning of shikantaza, I gave an example of one of my friends, and talking between the differences between my way and his way. Remember? Remember that talking is not a good way. *[Some laughter.]* No, don't repeat me, don't imitate me. I really reflect upon myself. It's not a good way. 

Whatever reason I say that that is not right. Even though I try to explain the Buddha's teachings with a really humble and modest spirit, and comparing with others, I don't think humbleness is always humble. Okay? *[Laughter.]* Remember, the human mind is very delicate. Do you understand? I reflected upon myself right after talking, and then, “My goodness! Oh, excuse me.” So I have to confess in front of you. I apologize. So, don't repeat me in that way, alright? 

Mind is really working very delicately and sneaking in always. *[Laughter.]* Apparently it seems to be pretty good, you know? “Katagiri, you are pretty humble...” No way. It’s really sneaky. So remember, I can tell you, even though you express yourself in a humble way or modest way, that is called arrogance of humbleness. Remember it! There still is arrogance there. Alright? 

That's what the Buddhism says. In total,  there are seven kinds of arrogance. *[He laughs.]* So, arrogance is not only arrogance, you know? *Seven* kinds. Including the humble, including the wise men, and whatever you say. That is really humble. If you emphasize so much poor, and then the poor is pretty good – “I am poor,” you know – so that is pretty nice, but you think it's really arrogant.

So, the humble, if you express yourself in humble way, it's very nice, but you should remember, still there is arrogance of the humbleness. 

So, I apologize. Don't repeat in that way. 

So that's why, there is an old saying that if you [...] and your virtue, then naturally you are repeated [by]. That's why Buddhist monks lived in the heart of the mountains. Do you remember? [...] you know. 

#### 41:51

Next:

> Do not imitate others' faults; just cultivate virtue.

So, cultivate virtue is, do your best to take care of life, so as not to miss this chance to manifest Buddha. Completely beyond humble or arrogant, just quietly, you should do it. 

It's not showy. Very quietly, all you have to do is, just follow the Buddhist way. And that is to cultivate virtue. 

So, zazen is pretty nice, because even if you are successful in doing shikantaza, this sesshin or whenever, no one gives you applause, no one gives you a prize, or sweet candy. Only [in] that moment you can feel good – but the next moment, you have to throw away the sweet candy called “enlightenment.” *[He chuckles.]* So no applause, no sweet candy come from anybody – from others, or from yourself either. That's wonderful, anyway. 

But, nevertheless, you have to continue to do this. That's why in the *Hokyo Zanmai*, *Jewel Mirror Samadhi*, the last paragraph says, “Like a fool, like an idiot, you achieve just a continuation of practice.” Just like a fool and an idiot, because there is no reward, no prize. With no prize, no reward, no one wants to do things, but zazen is a practice you have to do like that. So that's why you must be naturally a fool and an idiot. You cannot handle yourself or zazen in common sense; no. You just continue to do it. 

So, very naturally, it is a way to cultivate virtue. 

Okay, and then the last sentence I told you already: 

> Buddha prohibited wrongdoing, but did not say to hate wrongdoing. 

Again and again, you should remember this. Around your life, there are lots of wrongdoings, but don't handle wrongdoing with hatred. Deal with wrongdoing, hate, evil, or whatever, with compassion. [Remember to] walk hand in hand together, in peace and harmony.
