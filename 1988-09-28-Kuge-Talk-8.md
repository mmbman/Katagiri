## September 28, 1988 Dharma Talk by Dainin Katagiri Roshi

### Transcript

**This transcript is in rough draft stage.**

<p align="center" style="font-style: italic">

#### 0:00

> An ancient said, *The udumbara flower blooms in fire*. So the udumbara flower always blooms in fire. If you want to know *fire*, it is where the udumbara flower blooms. You should not, by clinging to views of humanity or views of heaven, fail to learn about *in the fire*. If you would doubt it, you should also doubt that lotus flowers grow in water; you should also doubt that there are flowers on branches. Also, if you must doubt, you should doubt the structure of the material world. Even so, you don’t doubt.
> 
> If one is not a Buddha or Zen adept, one does not know *when a flower blooms the world comes into being*. *A flower blooms* is *in front, three by three, in back, three by three*. In order to fulfill this number of members, all things are assembled and made grandiose. Invoking this principle, one can know the measure of spring and autumn.
>
> – From *Shōbōgenzō: Zen Essays by Dōgen*, translated by Thomas Cleary.

What Dōgen Zenji mentioned in the previous paragraph is [confirmed] here by a quotation of an ancient teacher, saying, "The udumbara flower blooms in fire." Then he, Dōgen Zenji, commented on this, "The udumbara flower always blooms in fire." 

So yesterday I mentioned *udumbara flower* is manifestation of the principle of absolute universality. And *fire* means, in this case, each aspect of everyday living. That is *fire*. So “udumbara flower always blooms in fire” means the truth is not far from everyday living; truth is always in everyday living. That's why here it says, "The udumbara flower *always* blooms in fire." Regardless of whether you are conscious of it or not, it always blooms in fire. 

“If you want to know *fire*, it is where the udumbara flower blooms.” And then he said it the opposite way: if you want to know fire. “If you want to know fire” means if you want to know everything which you can see, you can hear as an object: [...], table, flowers, pebbles, road, skies, trees, all the things you can see as an object. If you want to know the mountains as an object, you shouldn't see the mountain just as an object. The mountain is something more than mountain as an object in terms of human eyes. 

So if you read the *Sansuikyō*, “Sutra of the Mountains and Waters,” I think in this book, Sansuikyō, Scripture of the Mountains and Waters, page 89. In the first sentence he says:

> The mountains and waters of the immediate present are the manifestation of the path of the ancient Buddhas. Together abiding in their normative state, they have consummated the qualities of thorough exhaustiveness. Because they are events *prior to the empty eon*, they are the livelihood of the immediate present. 
>
> – From *Shōbōgenzō: Zen Essays by Dōgen*, translated by Thomas Cleary.

So when you see the mountains and the waters, Dōgen Zenji says, you should see them as the path of the ancient Buddha. You have to see the mountains as a Buddha. So see the mountains as a Buddha means not to think the mountains in your head only. But also you have to totally accept the mountains like that, with your heart and spirit. 

So Dōgen Zenji says an ancient buddha is a practicing buddha. *Practicing buddha* means if you want to manifest ancient buddha in the past, present, and future, they are manifested right in the midst of practice in motion – of doing *gassho*, of doing zazen, right there, before you poke your head into it. That is called *ancient buddha* in the past, present, and future. 

So *ancient buddha* is not the historical, mysterious buddha, who doesn’t exist *now*. So I think right in the midst of the practice from moment to moment in motion, buddhas and ancestors are manifested. That’s what Dōgen Zenji constantly says. That’s why it is not *your* business. Because if you poke your head [into it], it becomes your business. But just being alive right in the midst of practice in motion is not your business; you become completely returning to the silence, perfect silence. In the very incipient moment  – no world there, no room for your speculation to go, perception to go. Nothing. All you have to do is just be there and do it. 

This is called *undefiled practice*. *Undefiled* practice.

So that’s why you have to see and accept the mountains and waters as Buddha. 

> Together abiding in their normative state, ...

... Because they are abiding in the enormous world, the magnificent realm of the world which we have never seen. So mountains and rivers, all sentient beings live together. There is a same and one ground where all living beings live together. That is “abiding in their normative state.” 

> ... they have consummated the qualities of thorough exhaustiveness.

Very naturally mountains manifest their own quality [or] merit of *thorough exhaustiveness*, enormous magnificent state of existence. That’s why by mountains anyone can attain enlightenment. By mountains anyone can feel relief. Anyone can be free from suffering *by* mountains – by seeing mountains, by being with the mountains and waters. 

That’s why he says “they have consummated the qualities of thorough exhaustiveness.”

> Because they are events *prior to the empty eon*, they are the livelihood of the immediate present. 

So because they are events, mountains and rivers, why do we have to accept mountains and rivers as a buddha? Because they are nothing but the events *prior to the empty eons*, [that] means prior to germination of your speculation, germination of your human agency. Anyway that is *empty eons*: before you think, before your birth, anyway that is an event. [The] presence of the mountains are nothing but magnificent events, before the germination of human agency comes out. 

If so, what are they? What is an event, as mountains? That is “the livelihood of the immediate present.” So mountains are a magnificent event, eternal event, Buddha’s event. If so, we believe that it seems far from us. But it is not; eternity is *right now*. Eternity is right now. That’s why here it says, “the livelihood of the immediate present.” 

So in every aspect of everyday living, there is a magnificent event, as the mountains, as the water, as the *gassho*, as washing your face. That’s why we have to take best care of them.

That’s why Dōgen Zenji says if you want to know fire, everyday living, it is where the udumbara flower blooms. So if you cannot see it, anyway, be quiet, and be patient, and then try to know it, learn it. But the problem is that human beings are always irritated, or try to deny, if they don't know. If they don't know, they must be quiet, you know? Because they don't know. Even though they complain about it or even though they criticize it, that criticism doesn't make sense, because they don't know. 

For instance, Christians and other religious people criticizing Buddhism. When we tried to buy this land, many people criticized our group and Zen Buddhism, and they didn't want to sell this land to us. They complained about our existence, but how much do they know? They don't know about Zen. They know just a little bit through the mass media, the TV and the magazine, journalism, that's it. Nothing to be known. But nevertheless they complain and try to be against us. It doesn't make sense for me. 

So when you criticize other people and religions and the sciences, you must be careful. You should criticize *gently*. *[He laughs.]* Don't criticize *anything* arrogantly. Because you don't know, exactly. 

So,

> If you want to know *fire*, it is where the udumbara flower blooms. You should not, by clinging to views of humanity or views of heaven, fail to learn about *in the fire*. 

Don't see the mountains and waters in terms of human eyes; that is the view of humanity. Or don't see the mountains and rivers and pebbles and nature in terms of a heavenly eye. That means kind of, if you see the mountains and rivers [like] the heavenly beings, you always see a great halo around each mountain and each river. But that is excessive. That understanding is too much. 

But in terms of human eyes you don't see anything. That's why this is also too much. So that's why he said “you shouldn't by clinging to views of humanity or views of heaven.” If you do it, you fail to learn about anything as an object blooming in the fire. You cannot see it. 

So,

> If you would doubt it, you should also doubt that lotus flowers grow in water; you should also doubt that there are flowers on branches. Also, if you must doubt, you should doubt the structure of the material world. 

I think if you start to doubt, I think everything becomes questionable, skeptical. Everything becomes questionable. Because you doubt why the lotus flower blooms in the muddy water. Why does it have beautiful flowers without [being] contaminated by the muddy water? We have lots of questions. And also we doubt why we become Americans and why you were born as Americans. You doubt. So everything becomes questionable. But no matter how long you question those things, you never know something clear. 

So that's why Dōgen Zenji says here, “If you would doubt it, you should also doubt that lotus flowers grow in water; you should also doubt that there are flowers on branches. Also if you must doubt, you should doubt the structure of the material world.” 

“Material world” means circumstances. So when you are born, already there are circumstances there. So from where do the circumstances come before you are born? Lots of questions come up. 

But anyway, whatever kind of question you have, you shouldn't doubt it. Accept mountains and rivers as a Buddha *anyway*, day to day. Udumbara flower blooms in the fire: this is the something you have to practice day to day. Otherwise, your heart can never be generous; otherwise your heart can never be compassionate enough to walk in peace and harmony with the people hand in hand. No; you cannot cultivate this kind of heart or spirit [otherwise], because the generosity of your heart or compassion of your heart is not created by discussion, or by the understanding of something philosophically or metaphysically, but by the actual practice. 

We do, when you go to bed you chant the verse and then go to bed. I think this is a very important practice. A very important practice. “As I go to bed tonight, I vow with all sentient beings to calm all things, making the mind clear and pure.” So at that time, sleeping is not the usual sleeping you can see as an object. If you chant, if you accept sleep activity called *sleeping in bed as a buddha*, then you can chant it. Then within in the fire of the sleeping, udumbara flower blooms. Intellectually we don't believe this, but this is day-to-day practice. And then you can cultivate *cause* of the buddha. That is called *bodhi-mind*. Day to day, you have to do it. 

We always try to take care of everything intellectually, or in common sense, always. If we do this, you can accept the monastic rules, and then you chant this verse before you go to bed, but you don't believe, because this practice is ridiculous! You know? But if you don't do it, I think there is no room for you to cultivate heart of compassion, or generosity, which is enough for you to walk with the people hand in hand peacefully. So that is a most important practice; that's why in the monastery we do always like this. 

So,

> Even so, you don’t doubt.

Just accept and just practice. 

I have done just like this for many, many years. When I went to Eiheiji monastery, I met Hashimoto Roshi. I didn't have any idea of Buddhism as a philosophy, as a religion. I didn't have anything, but I met Hashimoto Roshi in Eiheiji monastery and I was very impressed by his activities, actions from moment to moment. It was very pure and very impressive. I got a very strong shock from his walking, from his gassho, from his lying down, from his talking, from his drinking tea; whatever he did, there was something, something I couldn't explain. But if I sat by him, I felt relief, and I felt calm and peaceful. I didn't know why! So naturally I would like to imitate his activities and his behaviors, his *gasshos*, et cetera. And also he always emphasized that all monks should *gassho* properly; I gave instructions to you now. So he was very strict to do that. So I didn't know what it meant, but I just followed [it]. So for many, many years I did *gassho* like this, but now, *gassho* is just blooming flower of udumbara, exactly. *Gassho* I do is no longer some object far from me; it is nothing but manifestation of my whole life, past, present, future, including my parents and grandparents, and mountains, rivers, circumstances where I have grown up. Anyway, I felt this is *really* gassho I do, this is really bowing to Buddha. 

So we have to practice like this. Then, naturally, there is a cultivation of compassion or generosity, and wisdom there. 

Next, 

> If one is not a Buddha or Zen adept, one does not know *when a flower blooms the world comes into being*. 

So only Buddha and Zen adept knows this udumbara flower blooming in the fire. So if we say so, we are not Buddha, we are not Zen adept, so we don't know exactly. But “Buddha and Zen adept” mean is that they are manifested right in the midst of gassho, if you do [it] with wholeheartedness. [That means] day to day you become Buddha. Day to day from moment to moment you become Buddha, you become Zen teacher anyway. 

And then if you don't believe and doubt it, and then if you don't do it, there is no Buddha. If you do it, you become Buddha. 

So that's why here it says if one is not the Buddha or Zen adept, [it doesn’t] mean you are not Buddha. *We are Buddha*; you are Buddha already. 

So “one does not know *when a flower blooms the world comes into being*.” Then whole world comes into being. In other words, your heart, your affections, your emotions go into it; into the *gassho*, into the bows; into the trees, into the circumstances. Then the trees, nature, *gasshos* come into your heart. So vice versa; always there is communication there. 

Then that's why he says “the world comes into being.” When you cry, everything cries. When you smile, everything smiles. So even though you don't want to smile, smile it. Even though you want to cry, but be patient sometimes. You can cry; but sometimes you cannot cry. But if you want to cry, maybe secretly you can cry. *[He laughs.]* This is permissible, anyway. *[He laughs.]* But where is the place where crying is permissible secretly? I don't know where. But anyway, as best as you can, we shouldn't cry, even though we want to cry. Anyway smile, and be patient; otherwise everybody cries. [If] everybody cries, I think nothing to do for us, nothing to develop for anything. So we try to walk together, for long, everlastingly. In order to do this, I think we must be patient, we must practice patience, and zealousness, and offering six *paramitas*, anyway. 

So that is “the world comes into being.” 

> *A flower blooms* is *in front, three by three, in back, three by three*. 

I think if you look at the *Blue Cliff Record*, number 35. I don't have the *Blue Cliff Record* now, so I think you should look at it later. In Number 35, I think there is a story about Manjusri talking about eternity expressed as “in front three by three, in back three by three.” That is eternity; exhaustiveness, boundlessness. 

So *a flower blooms*, your presence, is *in front three by three, in back three by three*. Even though you see your front, it's eternal, it's boundless. Even though you see your back behind, it is eternal. So you cannot judge, evaluate your life quickly. Because within your life, there is *a flower blooms*; [the] udumbara flower blooms. 

So Flower Blooms is in front three by three, in back three by three. 

> In order to fulfill this number of members, all things are assembled and made grandiose. 

I think the, how can I say it, if one flower blooms, I think in the realm of *gassho*, I think the myriad, myriad beings exist. And also within each of the myriad, myriad beings there is the udumbara flower blooming. In other words, if you see the full moon, in the full moon there are many, many, myriad, myriad beings there. And simultaneously myriad, myriad full moons exist. In the whole world, you countless, numberless. And then all those myriad, myriad beings come together and create the world as grandiose, a magnificent world. This is called *the moon* you are looking at. This is called the subject of who looks at the moon. 

So it's magnificent world, magnificent being, magnificent present. That’s here it says, “in order to fulfill this number of members, all things are assembled and made grandiose.” So tables, notebooks, lamps, incense; whatever they are, they are magnificent world. 

> Invoking this principle, one can know the measure of spring and autumn.

Invoking this principle. This principle already mentioned, has been mentioned by Dōgen in this paragraph. In each aspect of human everyday living, udumbara flower always blooms, means a Buddha is manifested there. This is the principle. That principle is not something objective by which you can discuss, but something you have to *do*, practically, day to day. When you want to manifest Buddha, you should do *gassho* properly. If you want to know *gassho* in which Buddha exists, you have to do *gassho* properly. Anyway, do *gassho* properly. 

So it's completely beyond discussion. Just do it. 

There are lots of koans in the *Blue Cliff Record* or *Book of Equanimity*. I think *Book of Equanimity* will be published pretty soon by Zen Center in San Francisco. There is one koan: "All sentient beings return to one. Where does one return to?” And then Jōshū Zen Master says, "When I was in a city, I made a shirt. It weighed seven pounds." That’s what he said. 

So where does one return to? “All sentient beings return to one” or “all sentient beings originate from one” – we understand this one. But where does one return to? It means one returns to each aspect of everyday living, because udumbara flower blooms there. 

So where does *one* return to? *One* returns to each event of everyday living. That's it. 

So what does it mean? So he said, "I made a shirt. It weighs seven pounds." That means completely keep your mouth shut, you know? Because no matter how long you discuss about that shirt, seven pounds of shirt is ridiculous – it doesn't make sense for you. *It doesn't make sense* means keep your mouth shut and just be there. It means just do it. Just do it. 

[...] Jōshū Zen Master, a really great teacher, which occurs in *Blue Cliff Record* and *Book of Equanimity*. Or Dōgen Zenji really praised him deeply. Interesting story is that Baso Zen Master one day had the great chance to see the full moon at night with his disciples. Seido, one is his name is Seido. The other is Hyakujo Zen Master, Pai-chan. The other is Nansen Zen Master. Nansen is Zen Master who killed the cat. You know? Nansen. And then Baso Zen Master asked the disciples: “Say something about this full moon night.” And Seido says, “It is a great opportunity to practice.” *[He chuckles.]* You know, great night, great night. Do you understand what I mean? And Hyakujo Zen Master says, “It is a great night, great night to make offerings to the Buddha.” And Baso asked Nansen, “How about you?” And Nansen, excuse me, pushed [up his sleeves] and left. *[He laughs.]* He didn’t say anything. 

Do you understand this story? *[He laughs.]* 

Because Baso Zen Master asking, “What do you think about this full moon night?” Well, full moon night is not a particular being you can pick up. If you pick it up, it’s a full moon. But that full moon is already full moon limited by you. So Seido and Hyakujo already picked up the full moon by them, and then he commented on this, “This is a great opportunity to practice,” something like that. It’s not wrong, it’s right, it’s correct. But I think the point Baso tried to ask them is not something like that, but the full moon operating in the whole world, before you poke your head into it. That is the point Baso Zen Master tried to ask them. 

So that’s why Nansen completely pushed away his sleeves and stood up and left. It means nothing to say. It is already the practical world, practical world, you must live in day to day from moment to moment, with your mouth shut. That’s it. So how do we explain it? That’s [why] Nansen demonstrated by pushing [up] his sleeves and standing up and leaving. 

Another *koan* is Nansen killing the cat. You remember pretty well. Nansen killed the cat, and finally Nansen [said to] Jōshū Zen Master, “Oh, too bad you were not there.” You know, the day after discussing, Jōshū came back to the temple and Nansen met him, and then Nansen said to Jōshū, “Oh, too bad you were not there last night; if you were there, I wouldn’t haved killed the cat,” et cetera. And then do you remember what Jōshū Zen Master does? Yeah, he took away his sandals and put them on his head and left. *[Some laughter.]* And then people commented on this in many, many ways. *[He laughs.]* Because lots of questions there. “What do you mean, taking away the sandals and putting them on the head?” Is it criticizing Nansen, or criticizing Nansen’s behavior? Lots of discussion comes up from that story. But the point of Jōshū’s activity is that, I think Nansen and the cat and all monks living in where? Completely in silence, you know? Because cat is Buddha. Nansen is Buddha. Jōshū is Buddha, and all monks [are] Buddha who are discussing about the cats. Anyway, all Buddhas. 

So everything lives in the realm of silence, so called very incipient moment, called Buddha. At that time, nothing to say. 

Nansen said on purpose, “Oh, too bad you were not there.” But it doesn’t matter. Not for Nansen. It doesn’t matter whether Jōshū the master was there or Jōshū was not there. It doesn’t matter. He just said to Jōshū. 

And then Nansen said, “Too bad to kill the cats. I think if you were there, I wouldn’t kill the cat.” But killing the cat and also cat, all are anyway Buddha. Because by killing the cats, already something there. Monks be quiet, monks return to silence. Immediately monks were completely astonished to see Nansen’s behavior, killing the cat with one stroke, you know? Chinese [stories] really demonstrated very exaggerated like this, you know? But don’t imitate that. *[Laughter.]* If you imitate it, you are immediately put in jail. So don’t imitate it. This is a Chinese demonstration, anyway. 

But that is a very interesting human heart there. Very compassion there, very generosity there. Even though they killed the cat, Jōshū doesn’t criticize this; still he accepted this behavior in terms of Buddha’s eyes. And then, he handles this; he accepts this event with his hand, softly, compassionately. Like holding the bunch of thread with knots, [as] I mentioned before. If you hold hard, you create more knots. So you have to accept it softly, gently and work on it, one by one. 

And then, Jōshū said, Jōshū took [off] his sandle and put on it on his head. That means completely beyond speculation, beyond speculation. What Nansen did, or what the monks did, whatever it is, all returned to silence. At that time, it is Buddha. 

*[Tape change.]*

I think in terms of human eyes, killing a cat is called criminal. Anyway, a certain crime. So even Nansen, as a Zen adept, should take responsibility for it. So that is being against Buddha’s precepts, anyway. 

So Dōgen Zenji mentions [this] in the *Record of Things Heard*. So, this is very interesting. But anyway, Jōshū sees this event in terms of Buddha’s eye, that’s why he did like this. 

So same thing here. Those stories are exactly the same. 

That’s why “invoking this principle, one can know the measure of spring and autumn.” That’s why first you have to invoke this principle, and then you have to see the spring, autumn, and summer and winter. Otherwise, summer, spring, autumn, winter, cannot be dealt with compassionately, kindly, in terms of Buddha’s eye. So first we have to invoke this principle. 

That is completely beyond speculation; it is something you *do*. 

So for instance, if you see Minnesota climate in winter, you hate it. But in terms of Buddha’s eye, don’t hate it *immediately*; first invoke this principle. First, accept... softly and gently, compassionately, severe winter, and work on it. That’s it! This is a practice for us. 

But still emotions are coming up, emotions are coming up. That’s why you have to continually invoke this principle, invoke this principle, and do it, do it, do it. Day to day, you have to do it.

Okay. Do you have questions? 

#### 48:53

**Question:** You were talking about events “prior to the empty eon.”

**Katagiri Roshi:** Mm-hmm.

**Questioner:** You said that meant before your birth?

**Katagiri Roshi:** Mm-hmm.

**Questioner:** What do you mean by “before your birth.”

**Katagiri Roshi:** “Before your birth” means before the birth of [your] parents. Before birth means everything becomes one. That moment. Everything becomes one. 

When I see this table, *I* see this table, but actually I see this table and many beings. I see many beings. So many beings come and see it. At that time, how can I see them? That is a unity, same and one ground. That is “before your birth.” In other words, the very incipient moment of seeing this table. At that time, that incipient moment includes all sentient beings there, simultaneously. Beyond your speculation. 

That’s why “beyond your birth.” Before your birth. That is “empty eons.” Beyond empty eons.

So in the realm of *beyond your birth*, there is only your actual practice in motion, day to day. Because if you think of it, you never know it. So first, we have to... well, *communicate*; we have to open our heart and then *communicate* with that – the very incipient state of the moment. We have to go into it, go into it, constantly. And then there are emotions, and affections, and intellectual things coming up, naturally.
