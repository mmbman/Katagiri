For instance, if in your life there are three important things: [shelter], food, and clothes. So, according to the logic, first of all you should keep the food, clothes, and housing. Without this, you cannot survive. They are very common. Do you understand? But sometimes not! Even if you don’t have food and clothes and a house, but actually there is Katagiri there, Katagiri right there. Very naturally, Katagiri starts to find the food. Do you understand? Find the food, find the clothes. So very naturally logic comes up. Within the total concentration of the living, there is no trace of perception of the food, or clothes, or house. Complete beyond. Just living.  But if you do it, if you are exactly right in the middle of that situation, very naturally you start to find food. So, naturally, logical comes up. Alright? That is human civilization, human culture. 

So, that’s why Buddhism always emphasizes, “let’s see that’s reality as it is,” which is spinning constantly. Because this is the best way to know the self as it really is, not as a perception. So that’s why planting into zazen, zazen teaches you very naturally like this. 

So you can see many things in zazen. And already you have vitality, energies, and powers. And also you have a computer, so-called way seeking mind. And then it’s already started to work. Then you sit down, simultaneously huge information comes up. You don’t know how to collect the data. And also you don’t know how huge the universe is. So still you can see how many beings you don’t know, through zazen. 

So very naturally you know how small your life is, and how big the universe is. Never-the-less, you want to know what they are. This is a [...]. 

But the important point is the self. Self is also self; you don’t know the self exactly. Within the self, there are lots of things you don’t know. So when you sit down exactly, there are many things that come up. But whatever information you can collect or you cannot collect, all you have to do is let’s spin as zazen. That is the best way to learn the self as it is. 

So that’s why I say zazen is just like a house; the *whole* house. But if you believe zazen is the right concentration on the breath, that is you are always concentrating on one pillar, which is important pillar for the house. But in the [full] house, much information is there. 

But you have to be the whole house. So for this, zazen is the *whole house*, exactly. 

And then you can get the many informations, so that’s why you are confused. But it’s not confused, it’s natural. It’s natural. 

So whatever kind of information you can collect or you cannot collect, anyway, from the beginning to end you must be the whole house. This is so-called the way to learn the self as it is. And that is the cognition of wisdom – no, the wisdom of enlightenment. Alright? So that is called *prajnaparamita*. 

So even though you don’t know what the *prajnaparamita* is, you can do it, practically. That’s why we do it like that.

And then we set up these circumstances and do zazen. So in zazen, I don’t think we should play with worldly affairs based on human cognition. So in the zazen, all you have to do is just spin. That is the best way to know the self as it is. Usually that is the point we think. That’s what we have to know about that. That is zazen. 

And that is a this circumstances which causes zazen to work well. This is this situation, monastery so-called. [Withdrawing] from the world, and then come here and do zazen. But nevertheless we don’t do it, because we waste the time of zazen thinking the worldly affairs. That time, that is “how sad it is.” That’s why Dogen then says, 

> Is it not sad if you waste this time, concerning yourself with secular affairs?

I don’t mean that secular affairs are something wrong. No. It is important, because we live like that. But why do you come here, why do you do zazen? Because in the secular world we realize there is something missing: to know the self that it really is, not as a conception. That’s why this is zazen, this is the circumstances, this is the place, so-called “monastery.” And put aside all worldly affairs, and then you come here, throwing away jobs and many things, and then you come here and you sit down. Why is it you have to spend your time to think woldly affairs? If you waste the time of zazen thinking of worldly affairs, it is not necessary to come here! To do zazen. Do you understand that one? 

Anyway, be careful of this. That’s why I say, even though you attain enlightenment, “Fine, sit down.” Even though you are confused: “Fine, sit down.” Please sit down! Because this is the direct way to jump into, plunge into. Cause and effect becomes exactly one. There is no perception there. 

Through that, many thoughts, human thoughts, human cultures, and the knowledge and the science come up from that situation. But we forget that one, always. 

That’s why if you come here, please, when you do zazen, *just do zazen*. Even though you think of worldly affairs, but don’t *meddle* with it. Emotions, et cetera, that’s fine! But that is natural stuff, so please concentrate on doing zazen. Tired, whatever it is – just do it. 

That’s why Dogen Zenji talks about it like this. 

And also, this chance you come here this time is very rare. You cannot repeat the same thing next time. When you come next time, the situation is completely different, your experience will be completely different. 

So this time, this opportunity is very important. That’s why Dogen Zenji says: 

> Now is the time for the fire on your head to be wiped out.
>
> – From “Regulations for the Auxiliary Cloud Hall (Jūundō-shiki)” in *Moon in a Dewdrop*, edited by Kazuaki Tanahashi.

That is a very emergency case. 

And also look at yourself. Nevertheless, even though you understand this emergency case, still we don’t use the time of zazen as it is – planting into the Buddhist world, planted into the self as it is, based on the dharma, the universe. That is a zazen self. Total communication there.

#### 33:29

And then, Dogen Zenji says,

> The impermanent is unreliable.
>
> – From “Regulations for the Auxiliary Cloud Hall (Jūundō-shiki)” in *Moon in a Dewdrop*, edited by Kazuaki Tanahashi.

This is the observation of impermanence, exactly. Because if you live like that, very naturally impermanent, how impermanent, working very quickly, swiftly. How changeable we are – including your mind, your body. 

> No one knows where and when this dew-like existence will drop into the grass. 

What kind of grass your dew drops in? One of the six realms of existence? Hell, hungry ghost, fighting spirit, emotional fighting, human beings? Bodhisattvas, buddhas, sravakas? What kind of grasses does your dewdrop drop in? That is everything is changing, constantly. Your life is just a dewdrop. Where are you dropping it? What kind of grass do you want to drop in? Hungry ghost? [No one.] Right there. 

So if you do zazen, zazen is exactly Buddha zazen. Okay? Buddha zazen. Because you can exactly communicate with the self which is the universe. 

But you don’t notice, you have never experienced this, that’s why you don’t believe. If you don’t believe, anyway, you must be first docile: docile toward truth. And then, conscientious. I don’t mean you should blind. You’ll never [should] be blind. If you are docile for the truth, learning the self, naturally your eyes, your knowledge opens, naturally. 

That is zazen: a very strange practice, unique Buddhist practice. People don’t know exactly what it is. But if you do it, exactly you can learn. 

So no one knows where your body, your mind, is really changing, dew drops. Because if you ... recklessly, very naturally you create a noisy word.
